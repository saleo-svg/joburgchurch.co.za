### Article body

I lead a small Bible study in Parkmore, Sandton, that meets online every Wednesday evening. We have fifteen people on a busy night. We are not a megachurch. Most weeks, I am reading the same passage two or three times because the room has newcomers.

About two years ago, one of our regulars — a young woman in her twenties — asked me a question after Bible study that I still think about.

"Uncle, can I bring my friend next week? She does not speak much English but she wants to come."

"Sure," I said. "Bring her."

She came. She sat at the edge of the room, near the door, and listened for forty minutes. After Bible study, she asked her friend to translate a sentence for her.

"Can I ask, is there any Korean class for expats? I am from Seoul. I have not taught anything since I left home."

There is a long pause you can imagine after that kind of question. I am a Bible teacher, not a Korean-language school. I do not teach Hangul. I have never taught Hangul. But she had asked politely, and she was sitting at the end of the row, and the room was still.

"We can try," I said. "What would it look like?"

What it looked like, in the end, was a Sunday afternoon, in the same room we already had booked. She would teach Hangul from the first consonant to a small conversation, in twelve weeks. We would ask for nothing. We would tell anyone who walked past that there was a free class.

What I want to say, in this short piece, is that this is how a lot of small community ministry begins. It does not begin with a strategy meeting. It begins with someone at the door asking something the church has never done before.

The first Sunday of the Korean class, four people came. Three of them were Korean. One was a South African woman who had been dating a Korean man for six years and wanted to say something on his mother's birthday that was not "annyeonghaseyo".

By the third Sunday, the class had eight people. By the sixth, twelve. Some came back week after week; some drifted off. The teacher flew home to Seoul for her brother's wedding in March, and another regular stepped in to keep the lesson running in her absence. People who taught Korean and people who only learned Korean and people who were just curious — they became, somehow, the same group of people around a small table on a Sunday afternoon.

We are still a Bible study first. Wednesday online, 7:30pm, we open a chapter and read it slowly. The Korean class is on Sunday afternoon because the room is empty then. There is no grand plan to make us into a language school. There is no clever marketing effort. There is a room, a table, and a need that turned out to be larger than I had imagined.

Two things I have learned, in case this is useful to anyone reading.

**One:** A free thing, with no requirement to stay afterwards, is the most honest kind of invitation a church can offer. People who come for the Korean class do not come to be preached at. They come for the class. Some of them ask, weeks later, about the Bible study. Some of them do not, and that is fine too.

**Two:** The thing that holds a small church together is rarely the sermon. It is the willingness to do something small on behalf of someone who is new. Most of what we do in Sandton has nothing to do with theology. It has to do with making a room feel less strange for the people who walk into it.

If you lead a small Bible study in a suburb anywhere, this is your permission to try the strange thing your regulars keep asking about. It will not work every time. It will probably change the shape of your church in ways you cannot plan for.

It certainly changed ours.

— Sim, Parkmore, Sandton

### Article body

I have been helping run a youth programme in Johannesburg for about three years now. It is called UseCan, which is a small South African inside-joke that means "you can". I have written about it before; I will not belabour the name.

Last summer we did our first winter camp. Forty teenagers, three days, Heia Safari Ranch outside Magaliesburg. We drove up on a Friday afternoon in two hired kombis, with the windows down and the cooler box sliding across the floor on every turn. By the time we arrived, one of them had already lost his phone and another had made friends with a farmer's dog at the petrol station.

I want to be honest about what I was worried about, because I think most youth leaders are worried about the same things and most articles pretend we are not.

I was worried that no one would talk. I was worried that the teenagers who had never been to camp before would feel awkward for the entire weekend. I was worried that the boys and the girls would split into two silent groups. I was worried about the late-night walks. I was worried about the Bible study time slot being the bit everyone dreaded.

Here is what actually happened.

**The Bible study time slot was the bit everyone waited for.** Not because we are exceptional teachers. We are not. We are two adults reading a Gospel passage slowly and asking what is difficult about it. But the teenagers had so many questions about Jesus that they had never been given room to ask, that when we gave them room, they filled it. On the second night, a fifteen-year-old asked a question about forgiveness that made three of the adults in the room go quiet for a minute.

**The icebreaker I had planned did not work. The one I had not planned did.** I had a name game with a ball. It lasted four minutes. What lasted ninety minutes was the conversation that started when one of the boys explained gogo's cooking to a kid from Cape Town. Everyone wanted to know what gogo meant. Then everyone wanted to explain their own grandmother's word for "grandmother". By 11pm we had a working glossary of South African family terms across four languages.

**Some teenagers came for the wrong reason and stayed for the right one.** A parent had made a reservation; a school friend had said yes; someone needed a Saturday job reference and figured volunteering looked good. I stopped trying to guess who was "really there". Most of them, in their own time, told us why.

**The drive home was the moment.** On the way up we had a kombi of strangers. On the way down, we had a kombi of people who had shared a weekend. Three of the kids who barely spoke on Friday were singing together on Sunday afternoon. Two of them are now leading the Wednesday youth Bible study.

If you are a youth leader anywhere, I would tell you this. The camp is not the curriculum. The camp is the room. Once teenagers trust that the room is safe, they will do the rest. The work for us was not to make the weekend spectacular. It was to make it consistent — same rules, same bedtime, same adult in the same room — for three days in a row.

That, I think, is the part we forget. They do not remember the hikes. They remember that someone was there every morning and looked like they wanted to be.

— Dora, Parkmore, Sandton
