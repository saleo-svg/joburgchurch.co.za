## The Month Johannesburg Changes Clothes

Every September, Johannesburg changes clothes. Not literally — although some of us do. The corporate office in Sandton is a little quieter on the 24th. The radios in the taxis play a different kind of music. The malls put up a different kind of poster. The aunties on the WhatsApp group send the same message twice — once in Zulu, once in English, once in Afrikaans. Somebody at the braai brings a flag. Somebody else brings a different flag, and the conversation around the fire gets longer than anyone planned.

September is Heritage Month in South Africa. It has been since 1996, when the new parliament chose to fold the old Shaka Day and the new democratic celebration into a single day, September 24th, and call it Heritage Day. The point was not to replace one memory with another. The point was to make room for many memories at once — and to do it without anyone having to choose which one was the real one.

The day is complicated. Every South African knows this. The day is also beautiful. Every South African knows this too.

This post is for the Christian in Johannesburg who wants to mark September well, who wants to be honest about the weight of the month, and who wants to know what the Bible has to say about memory, identity, and the hope that outlives both.

## What the Bible Actually Says About Remembering

The Bible has a great deal to say about remembering. More than most Christians realise. The Bible does not treat memory as a private habit. It treats it as a public discipline.

**Deuteronomy 6:12** — *Be careful that you do not forget the Lord, who brought you out of Egypt, out of the land of slavery.*

**Joshua 4:6-7** — *In the future, when your children ask you, 'What do these stones mean?' tell them that the flow of the Jordan was cut off before the ark of the covenant of the Lord... These stones are to be a memorial to the people of Israel forever.*

**Psalm 78:4** — *We will tell the next generation the praiseworthy deeds of the Lord, his power, and the wonders he has done.*

**Psalm 105:5** — *Remember the wonders he has done, his miracles, and the judgments he pronounced.*

**1 Corinthians 11:24-25** — Jesus, at the last supper, took the bread, broke it, and said, *'Do this in remembrance of me.'* Then he took the cup, and said the same.

What you notice is that the Bible treats forgetting as a serious spiritual danger. In Deuteronomy 6, the warning is that the people will forget the Lord when they settle in the new land, when the houses are built, when the cisterns are full, when the children have grown up not knowing the journey. The cure is memory. Not private memory. Public, communal, generational memory. The stones at the Jordan. The bread on the table. The stories told at bedtime.

In the same way, September is not a private thing for the Christian in Johannesburg. It is a public thing. The Christian who engages with Heritage Month is doing something the Bible has been asking the people of God to do for three thousand years.

## What the Bible Says About Identity That Is Given, Not Earned

The other thing the Bible has a great deal to say about is identity. The Bible never treats identity as something we have to build ourselves. The Bible treats identity as something given — given by God, named by God, confirmed by God.

**Genesis 1:27** — *So God created mankind in his own image, in the image of God he created them; male and female he created them.*

**Genesis 17:5** — *No longer will you be called Abram; your name will be Abraham, for I have made you father of many nations.*

**1 Peter 2:9** — *But you are a chosen people, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God's special possession, that you may declare the praises of him who called you out of darkness into his wonderful light.*

**Galatians 3:28** — *There is neither Jew nor Gentile, neither slave nor free, nor is there male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus.*

This matters in September for two reasons.

First, because the Bible recognises that the Christian has multiple identities that do not cancel each other out. A Christian in Sandton in September is simultaneously a child of God, a South African, a Zulu or Xhosa or Sotho or Tswana or Afrikaner or English or Indian or coloured person, a member of a family with its own story, a worker in a particular industry, a neighbour in a particular street. None of these is the only identity. None of them cancels the others. The Bible makes room for all of them.

Second, because the Bible refuses to let any of those identities become the only thing that matters. In Galatians 3, the identity in Christ Jesus is the identity that holds the others together without competing with them. The Christian in Johannesburg does not have to choose between being South African and being Christian. The Christian in Johannesburg does not have to choose between being Zulu and being a Christian. The Bible makes room for the full complexity of a September afternoon.

## Why September Is Harder Than Most Months in Johannesburg

September is harder than most months in Johannesburg for three reasons.

### 1. The Questions Are Public

Most months, the questions about who we are and where we come from are private questions. They sit in the back of the family car or at the edge of a WhatsApp group. September makes them public. The questions are on the radio. The questions are in the office. The questions are at the braai. The questions come from the children, who have heard something at school and want to know what to make of it. The questions come from older relatives, who are carrying their own answers and want to know what the next generation will do with them.

The Christian in Johannesburg in September has to be ready for the public question. The Christian in Johannesburg in September has to know what they believe and why they believe it. This is not a season for vague answers.

### 2. The History Is Heavy

September is a month in which the history of the country is unusually present. For some South Africans, the history is the history of a kingdom that was here long before the colonial powers arrived. For some, the history is the history of the dispossession. For some, the history is the history of the struggle. For some, the history is the history of a community that arrived as slaves and built a life anyway. For some, the history is the history of a community that arrived as settlers and built a different life. For some, the history is all of these at once.

The Christian in Johannesburg does not have to resolve the history. The Christian in Johannesburg does, however, have to acknowledge the history. The Bible is not naive about history. The Bible contains lament. The Bible contains protest. The Bible contains the long conversation between the people of God and the hard facts of their own past. The Christian in Johannesburg in September is allowed to do the same.

### 3. The Hope Is Easy to Lose

September is a month in which it is easy to lose hope. The news cycle does not stop for Heritage Day. The load-shedding schedule does not pause. The unemployment number does not change. The crime statistics do not improve. The country is what it is, and the country is hard.

The Bible does not promise the Christian an easier country. The Bible promises the Christian a different kind of hope. The hope is not that the country will become what it should become in the next news cycle. The hope is that the country is held by a God who is faithful across generations. The hope is that the bread on the table on Sunday is a sign of a different kind of kingdom.

## What the Sandton Bible Study Has Started Doing in September

In the last three Septembers, the Wednesday evening Bible study in Sandton has changed shape. Not a lot. Just enough. Here is what we have done, for what it is worth.

### We Open With a Psalm

For the first four Wednesdays of September, we open with one of the lament psalms. Psalm 74. Psalm 79. Psalm 137. We read it slowly. We let the room be quiet. We do not try to fix it. We let the Bible hold the heaviness for us before we ask the Bible to teach us.

This has been important. It has saved us from jumping straight into the comfortable parts of the Bible, the parts that feel easy in a Sandton September. The lament psalms are not comfortable. They are not meant to be. They are meant to make room for the real September.

### We Read a Passage About Another Heritage Month

The Old Testament has a number of heritage months. The Passover is one of them. The Feast of Tabernacles is another. The Jubilee year is another. For the four Wednesdays of September, we read one of these passages and ask the same three questions: what did this festival mean to the people who first kept it? what does it mean for us as South Africans today? what does it mean for us as the people of God in Johannesburg in 2026?

This has been a surprisingly rich exercise. The Israelites had a heritage month that was about remembering the exodus from Egypt. We have a heritage month that is about remembering many things at once. The questions are similar. The answers are different. The Bible is enough for both.

### We Invite Someone Older to Speak

For one Wednesday in September, we invite someone from the community who is older than most of us to share what they remember. A grandmother who remembers the location before the houses were built. A father who remembers the long drive to the farm. A pastor who remembers the church under the tree. We give them twenty minutes. We do not interrupt. We do not argue with what they remember. We listen.

This is not always easy. The older person sometimes says something we would not say. The older person sometimes remembers a country we did not live in. The older person sometimes asks a question we cannot answer. The Bible tells us to honour the older person. The Bible tells us to listen to them. We are trying to learn to do that in September.

### We End With Communion

For the last Wednesday of September, the night before Heritage Day itself, we end the Bible study with communion. Not because communion is a Heritage Day thing. Because communion is the great Christian practice of remembering, and September is the great South African practice of remembering, and the two fit together.

We read 1 Corinthians 11. We break the bread. We share the cup. We let the bread and the cup carry the history we cannot say out loud. The history of a country. The history of a community. The history of a Christian who is trying to follow Jesus in the middle of both.

## What the Bible Says About the Hope That Outlives the History

The Bible does not let September end without pointing forward. The Bible has a habit of taking the long view.

**Jeremiah 29:11** — *For I know the plans I have for you, declares the Lord, plans to prosper you and not to harm you, plans to give you hope and a future.*

**Romans 8:28** — *And we know that in all things God works for the good of those who love him, who have been called according to his purpose.*

**Revelation 21:1-4** — *Then I saw "a new heaven and a new earth," for the first heaven and the first earth had passed away... He who was seated on the throne said, "I am making everything new!"... 'He will wipe every tear from their eyes. There will be no more death or mourning or crying or pain, for the old order of things has passed away.'*

**Hebrews 12:1-2** — *Therefore, since we are surrounded by such a great cloud of witnesses, let us throw off everything that hinders and the sin that so easily entangles. And let us run with perseverance the race marked out for us, fixing our eyes on Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of faith.*

The hope the Bible offers in September is not the hope that the country will be sorted out by Heritage Day. The hope the Bible offers is the hope that the country is held by a God who has been faithful across all the Septembers that have come before, and will be faithful across the Septembers that are coming. The hope is not that we have remembered everything. The hope is that we have been remembered by God.
