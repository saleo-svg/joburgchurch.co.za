## The Bible Study That Started in a Kitchen in Orlando West

In 2021, a woman named Mamariti started a Bible study in her mother's kitchen in Orlando West, Soweto. She had been part of a church in the suburb for years but felt something was missing. She talked to her cousin. Her cousin talked to a friend. The friend talked to a neighbour. Six women started meeting on Saturday mornings at 9am.

Three years later, that kitchen Bible study has rotated between three different homes in Orlando West and Diepkloof, has a regular attendance of about fourteen women and four men, and has read through the entire book of Romans twice.

It is the most under-resourced Bible study we have ever visited. It is also one of the most faithful.

This post is about what we have learned in five years of visiting Bible studies in Soweto, Alexandra, Tembisa, and Mamelodi. It is not about what we have taught. It is about what we have been taught.

## What Township Bible Study Looks Like Up Close

If you have only ever been to a Bible study in the northern suburbs of Johannesburg, the first thing that surprises you about a township Bible study is the noise. There is no quiet kitchen in a township. There is a generator two streets over. There is a *shebeen* on the corner that gets louder as the morning goes on. There are children everywhere — yours, the neighbour's, the children who have heard there is food if they come to Bible study.

The second thing that surprises you is the welcome. Township hospitality is not the polite welcome of the suburbs. It is the welcome of someone who has very little and is determined you are going to have a plate of it. You are offered tea before you have put your bag down. You are offered *mogodu* if it is a Monday. You are offered a chair that has been carried out of someone's bedroom so you do not have to sit on a plastic one.

The third thing that surprises you, and this is the part that does the real work, is the depth. The Bible study in a township is not a polite discussion of the passage. It is a confrontation with the passage by people who have lived through things the passage is about. When the reading is from Exodus — the Israelites in bondage, the plagues, the long walk through the wilderness — you are sitting in a room with people whose grandmothers walked a longer wilderness. The questions they ask are not academic. They are the questions of people who have needed God to show up and who have sometimes had to wait.

## Five Things We Have Learned From Township Bible Study

### 1. The Bible Study Is the Building

In the suburbs, we tend to think of the building as primary and the Bible study as something that happens inside it. In the townships, the Bible study is primary. The building is whatever room is available. We have seen Bible studies meet in:

- A kitchen with a Formica table and six chairs
- A shebeen at 11am on a Saturday, when the chairs had been wiped down and the music turned off for two hours
- A *taxi* parked on a piece of open ground, with everyone sitting on crates around it
- The shaded area outside someone's RDP house, with a Bible balanced on a plastic chair
- A church building that has not had electricity for two years
- A school classroom borrowed from a sympathetic principal

The lesson for the rest of us is not that we should stop renting buildings. The lesson is that the Bible study is not the building. The Bible study is the people who have agreed to show up. The building is a container. The people are the content.

### 2. The Discussion Goes Longer Because the Stakes Are Higher

In the suburbs, a Bible study discussion often runs about forty-five minutes. In the townships, it runs for two hours, and could run for three. The reason is not that townships are slower. The reason is that the questions being asked are bigger.

When a woman in Mamelodi asks why God allows her son to be murdered in a hijacking, the answer is not a Sunday school answer. The answer is the kind of answer that takes an hour, and that is what the room gives her. The Bible study holds the space. It does not rush the question. It does not move on to the next point until the room has actually carried the weight of the one before.

The lesson for the rest of us is that the suburb's forty-five-minute discussion is not the gold standard. The two-hour discussion is the gold standard. The suburb is just in more of a hurry.

### 3. Hospitality Is Part of the Bible Study

In a township Bible study, hospitality is not a separate event that happens after the Bible study. Hospitality is the Bible study. The tea and the *vetkoek* and the *magaru* and the slice of bread with peanut butter — those are the sacraments of the gathering. To refuse them is to refuse the gathering.

We have learned to arrive early and stay late. We have learned that the meal is the time when the real conversation happens. We have learned that the most honest question someone will ask is usually asked while you are both standing at the kitchen sink drying mugs.

The lesson for the rest of us is that our Wednesday evenings need more food. We have started providing tea and rusks at our Saturday in-person evenings in Parkmore. It has changed the texture of the meeting. People stay longer. People talk to each other before and after the discussion. People bring things to share.

### 4. The Leader Does Not Need to Be a Theologian

In the suburbs, we tend to look for Bible study leaders with seminary degrees, with published books, with confident voices. In the townships, the leader is often a woman in her sixties who finished school in Standard 6 and has read the Bible three times through. She does not need a degree. She has the authority that comes from having walked with God for forty years through things we have not walked through.

The lesson for the rest of us is that we have been over-valuing credentials. The leader of a Bible study does not need to be the smartest person in the room. The leader needs to be the person who can hold the room. In the townships, that person is often the one with the most life in them, not the most letters after their name.

### 5. The Resources Flow Both Ways

This is the part that is hardest for the suburbs to hear. We tend to think of ministry in Soweto as something we go and do. We bring resources. We bring teaching. We bring curriculum. We bring the gospel. We come home.

The Bible studies we have visited in Soweto and Alexandra have reversed this. They have resources we do not have. They have depth. They have a kind of prayer life that is rarer in the suburbs. They have a willingness to sit with suffering that the suburbs have not had to develop. They have songs that the suburbs have not heard.

The lesson for the rest of us is that the resources should flow both ways. If a Bible study in Parkmore sends a team to a Bible study in Soweto and brings nothing back, the team has missed the point. The team should come back with a Psalm sung in seSotho they have never heard before. The team should come back with a way of praying they did not know. The team should come back humbled, and they should bring that humility back into the Parkmore Bible study on Wednesday.

## How to Visit a Township Bible Study Well

If you are part of a suburban Bible study in Johannesburg and you want to visit a township Bible study — and we strongly recommend you do — here are some guidelines that have worked for us.

### Ask First, Do Not Just Show Up

This sounds obvious, but it is the most common mistake. A Bible study in the townships is not a tourist attraction. It is a community. Call ahead. Ask when they would like visitors. Ask if there is anything you should bring. Ask if there is anything you should not bring.

### Bring Something, But Not a Sermon

The right thing to bring is food. A pack of rusks. A tray of *koesisters*. A few bottles of juice. A bag of oranges. The wrong thing to bring is a sermon, a curriculum, or an offer to teach.

You are going to listen. You are not going to teach. If, after an hour, you are asked to say something, say something short. Otherwise, listen.

### Stay for the Meal

If the Bible study has food after, stay for the food. Do not check your watch. Do not leave early. The meal is the most important part of the gathering. The meal is where you will have the conversation that you will remember for the rest of your life.

### Be Honest About Where You Are From

Do not pretend you are from the township. Do not pretend you understand what it is like. You do not. Be honest about that. The Bible study community will respect the honesty more than they will respect the pretending.

### Follow Up

A single visit is good. A relationship is better. If you visit a township Bible study once, call the leader the next week. Ask how the Saturday went. Ask if you can come again. Ask if there is a way to serve — not teach, serve. Carry chairs. Bring more food. Help clean up.

### Do Not Take Photos Without Asking

This is basic, but worth saying. The Bible study community is not a backdrop for your social media. Ask before you take a photo. Accept the no.

## What We Are Trying to Build Across Johannesburg

Our Bible study in Parkmore is in the suburbs. The Bible studies we have visited in Soweto, Alexandra, Tembisa, and Mamelodi are not in the suburbs. Both are Bible studies. Both are doing the same work — a few people, a passage, a willingness to keep going.

What we are trying to build, slowly, is a Bible study community across Johannesburg that knows the Bible studies in the townships are not outreach projects. They are sister communities. They are equals. They have things to teach us that we cannot teach them.

If you are part of a Bible study in Johannesburg, in Cape Town, in Durban, in Pretoria — please, visit a Bible study in a township near you. Sit in the chair. Drink the tea. Stay for the meal. You will not leave unchanged.
