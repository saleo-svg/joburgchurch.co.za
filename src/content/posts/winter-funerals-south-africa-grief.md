## The Saturday in June

It was a Saturday in mid-June, two years ago. The temperature in Johannesburg was four degrees when I woke up. There had been frost on the cars in Parkmore. The kind of cold that makes the air burn when you breathe.

I drove to Soweto that morning. A member of our Wednesday Bible study community had lost her mother during the week. The funeral was at a church in Meadowlands. I arrived at 7am. By 8am, the church was full. By 9am, the church was overflowing. By 10am, the service had started, and the people who could not fit inside were standing outside in the cold, their breath visible in the morning air.

I am writing this post in July, in the middle of the South African funeral season. The funeral season is a thing. It has been a thing in this country for as long as most of us can remember. Every June, July, and August, the funeral homes in Johannesburg, Cape Town, Durban, and Pretoria are at full capacity. The weekend programmes in Soweto, Tembisa, Mamelodi, and Khayelitsha are full of services. The florists are at their busiest. The catering halls are booked months in advance. The undertakers work through the night.

This post is about what Christians in South Africa need to know about winter, grief, and the long walk through loss. It is not a how-to for grieving. It is a how-to for showing up for the people who are.

## Why Winter in South Africa Is the Funeral Season

Before the practical guide, a few words on why winter is the funeral season. There are three reasons.

### The Cold

The South African winter is not a mild one. The Highveld can drop below zero at night. The township homes in Soweto and Tembisa are often poorly insulated. The older people in our communities, who already carry the accumulated illnesses of a lifetime, are particularly vulnerable. Pneumonia, flu, and the complications of chronic illness all peak in the winter months.

### The Calendar

Most South African families have traditionally buried their dead on Saturdays. The Saturday burial means the week before is for the viewing, the night vigil, and the funeral programme printing. The Saturday burial also means the mourner has to take a day off work, which adds the additional stress of lost income in a country where the unemployment rate is what it is.

### The Way South Africans Grieve

South Africans grieve communally. The funeral is not a private event. It is a public one. The wider family, the neighbours, the church community, the old school friends, the former colleagues — everyone shows up. The Saturday funeral is the largest weekly gathering in most South African communities during the winter months.

For the Christian in South Africa, the funeral season is one of the most demanding pastoral seasons of the year. It is also one of the most theologically important. If you want to understand the Bible's teaching on grief, you cannot do better than to spend a winter in a South African township.

## What the Bible Actually Says About Grief

The Bible has more to say about grief than most Christians realise. The Psalms are full of it. The prophets weep. Jesus wept at the tomb of his friend. The early church grieved for Stephen. The letters to the Thessalonians spend more time on the resurrection of the dead than on almost any other topic.

Here are five passages that the Bible study community in Parkmore has returned to most often over the years of accompanying grieving families in Johannesburg.

### Psalm 23:4 — "Even though I walk through the darkest valley, I will fear no evil"

This is the verse most people think of when they think of funeral psalms. It is also the verse most people misunderstand. It does not promise that the valley will not come. It promises that you will not walk through it alone. The Hebrew word for *"walk"* is the same word used for the slow, deliberate walk of a person who knows where they are going, even when they cannot see the path.

### Psalm 88 — The Psalm That Ends in Darkness

Psalm 88 is the only psalm in the Bible that does not resolve. It starts in darkness, walks through darkness, and ends in darkness. The last line is *"darkness is my closest friend."*

This psalm is important for South African Christians because most of our tradition has been taught to look for the resolution. The Psalm 23 promise. The *"all things work together for good"* promise. The *"joy comes in the morning"* promise. Psalm 88 is the biblical permission to grieve without resolving. Some winters do not end in spring. Some funerals do not end in comfort. Some grief does not wrap up in a year. The Bible knows this. Psalm 88 is the Bible's permission slip.

### Romans 8:26 — "The Spirit intercedes for us with groans that words cannot express"

This verse is one of the most under-quoted verses in the New Testament on the subject of grief. It says, in essence, that the Holy Spirit is praying for you when you cannot pray for yourself. The grief that is too deep for words is still heard. The prayer that does not come out is still prayed.

For the South African mourner who has lost the words, this verse is the most important one in the Bible. The Spirit is already praying for you. You do not have to find the words. The Spirit has them.

### 1 Thessalonians 4:13-18 — The Resurrection of the Dead

Paul's letter to the Thessalonians contains the most extended teaching on the resurrection in the New Testament. He writes it to a community that is grieving. He does not tell them to stop grieving. He tells them to grieve, but not as those who have no hope.

The distinction is important. The Christian is allowed to grieve. The Christian is also allowed to hope. The two go together. The winter of grief is real, and the spring of resurrection is also real. They are not contradictions.

### John 11:35 — "Jesus wept"

Two words. The shortest verse in the English Bible. It is also the verse that says the most about the Christian response to death. Jesus, standing at the tomb of his friend Lazarus, weeps. He does not weep because he is powerless. He is about to raise Lazarus from the dead. He weeps because he is present. He is with the mourners. He is feeling what they are feeling. The shortest verse in the Bible is the verse about presence.

## What to Do When Someone You Know Has Lost Someone

Here is what we have learned in ten years of accompanying grieving families in Johannesburg. None of it is dramatic. All of it is practical. All of it comes from the Bible's teaching on presence.

### Show Up at the Viewing, Even If You Did Not Know the Person Well

The viewing is the night before the funeral. In South African tradition, it is held at the family home or at the funeral home. It is the most intimate moment of the funeral week. Showing up at the viewing — even briefly, even for twenty minutes, even if you only knew the deceased from a distance — is one of the most powerful things a Christian can do.

Bring something small. A bottle of juice. A packet of rusks. A loaf of bread. The family will not eat for the next three days. The food you bring will be eaten at 11pm by one of the cousins.

### Show Up at the Funeral, Even If You Have to Take the Day Off

The funeral is on Saturday. Most South African employers will give you the day off for a funeral. If yours will not, ask. If you are self-employed, take the day anyway. Showing up at the funeral is one of the most concrete expressions of the Christian teaching on presence.

Dress appropriately. Sit at the back if you do not know the family. Stay for the whole service. Do not check your watch. Stay for the tea after the burial. The tea is when the real conversations happen.

### Show Up in the Week After

The week after the funeral is the week most people forget. The funeral is over. The food has been eaten. The flowers have wilted. The family is back at home, with the empty chair at the table, and the phone has stopped ringing.

The Christian who shows up in the week after is doing the most Christian thing possible. A phone call on the Wednesday after. A visit on the Saturday after. A WhatsApp message three weeks later asking how the family is. These small acts of presence are the Bible's teaching on love in its most practical form.

### Do Not Say the Wrong Things

There are a few things well-meaning Christians say at South African funerals that are not helpful. *"God needed another angel."* (No. God does not need angels.) *"They are in a better place."* (Maybe. The family wants them here.) *"It was their time."* (Death does not run on a schedule.) *"Be strong for the children."* (Be weak. The children will see you be weak, and they will learn that grief is allowed.)

The right things to say are simple. *"I am sorry."* *"I am here."* *"I do not have words."* *"Tell me about them."* The right things are the ones that make space for the person who is grieving to feel what they are feeling, without being corrected.

### Send the Family Something After the Funeral

A meal delivered three weeks after the funeral is more meaningful than the biggest flower arrangement the day of. A card on the one-month anniversary. A phone call on the six-month anniversary. The Christian tradition of remembering the dead at certain intervals is not morbid. It is one of the most pastoral things the church does.

The South African Christian community has been doing this for generations. The umsebenzi (the work) of accompanying the bereaved is part of the rhythm of the year.

### Pray for the Family in the Long Term

Most prayer lists in South African churches have names on them for a week after the funeral. By the second week, the names have moved on. By the third month, the names have been forgotten.

The Christian who keeps the names on the prayer list for a year, or two years, or for as long as it takes, is doing the long, slow work of Christian presence. The names stay on the prayer list because the grief stays in the body. The Bible knows this. The Psalms know this. The Bible study community in Parkmore knows this.

## A Short Pastoral Note for the Bible Study Leader

If you lead a Bible study in Johannesburg, or in any South African community, here is one thing to add to your Wednesday evening. Once a quarter, set aside the last fifteen minutes of the Bible study to remember the people in your community who have lost someone in the last three months. Read a Psalm. Pray for the names. Send a card to one of the families the next week.

The Bible study community that remembers the dead is the Bible study community that holds the living.

## A Winter Prayer for the Grieving

If you are grieving this winter, or if you are walking alongside someone who is, here is a prayer.

> *God of the cold months,*
>
> *We pray for the families who have lost someone this winter. For the mothers who buried a child. For the children who buried a parent. For the husbands and wives who are learning the new shape of an empty bed. For the friends who thought they had more time.*
>
> *We do not ask that you would explain. We do not ask that you would fix. We ask that you would stay.*
>
> *Stay with the one who cannot sleep. Stay with the one who cannot eat. Stay with the one who has run out of words for the prayer. Stay with the one who is angry at you. Stay with the one who is angry at themselves.*
>
> *In a winter that has no end in sight, be the winter that holds. In a funeral season that has been going on for years, be the funeral that finally stops. In a grief that does not resolve, be the grief that does not have to.*
>
> *We pray in the name of the one who wept at the tomb, who stood in the graveyard, who said "I am the resurrection and the life."*
> *Amen.*
