## The Wednesday Night With Eleven Languages

Last Wednesday, in the middle of our Bible study in Parkmore, we paused for the part of the evening where people are free to pray out loud in whatever language they choose. In the space of about four minutes, we heard prayers in:

- English (twice)
- isiZulu
- Afrikaans
- seSotho
- isiXhosa
- Setswana
- isiNdebele
- Sepedi
- Portuguese (from a Brazilian member)
- Korean
- French (from a member who works for a French NGO)

Eleven languages. One room. One prayer.

This is the ordinary Wednesday reality of Bible study in Johannesburg. The city has always been multilingual, but the particular mix of languages has shifted in the last fifteen years. The old Johannesburg had English, Afrikaans, and the official African languages of the people who lived in the townships. The new Johannesburg has all of those, plus Portuguese from Mozambique and Angola, French from the DRC and the francophone African countries, Swahili from East Africa, Arabic from North Africa and the Levant, Korean and Mandarin from East Asia, Hindi and Gujarati from South Asia, and Yoruba and Igbo from West Africa.

If your church or Bible study in Johannesburg is still operating in English only, you are not reaching the city you are actually in.

## What Multilingual Worship Actually Means

Multilingual worship is not the same as bilingual worship. A bilingual service alternates between two languages. A multilingual service holds multiple languages at the same time, and the work of the service is to make space for all of them.

Multilingual worship has at least three layers.

### The Visible Layer: What People Hear

The visible layer is the language of the songs, the prayers, the readings, the sermon. Most Johannesburg churches operate primarily in one or two languages at this level. The big Afrikaans churches use Afrikaans and English. The big English-speaking churches use English and sometimes isiZulu. The Catholic and Anglican churches often use English, isiZulu, and seSotho.

A truly multilingual service does something different. The reading is in one language. The sermon is in another. The songs rotate through three or four languages. The prayers are invited in any language, and the response is silence while the prayer is being prayed.

### The Hidden Layer: What People Feel

The hidden layer is the feeling of being welcomed in your own language. When a prayer is prayed in isiXhosa in a room of mostly English speakers, and the room holds the silence respectfully, the isiXhosa speaker feels something that no amount of English welcome can replicate. They feel seen. They feel like they belong. They feel like the room is large enough to hold them.

This is the work of multilingual worship. It is not the words. It is the welcome.

### The Theological Layer: What the Bible Says About It

The theological layer is the Bible's teaching about language. The Old Testament begins with a story of one language at Babel, then ends with a vision of many languages at the throne (Revelation 7:9-10). Pentecost reverses Babel. The Holy Spirit gives the disciples the ability to speak in the languages of the nations gathered in Jerusalem. The early church is multilingual from its first day.

A South African church that takes the Bible seriously cannot ignore the multilingual character of the early church. The first worship service in Acts 2 was in at least fifteen languages. Our Wednesday evening in Parkmore, with eleven, is closer to the first-century model than most of our churches realise.

## The Practical Challenges of Multilingual Worship

Multilingual worship is harder than monolingual worship. Anyone who has tried to lead it will tell you that. Here are the main challenges, and how we have seen Bible study communities in Johannesburg navigate them.

### Translation Time

If the sermon is in English and someone needs it in isiZulu, you need either a translator or a longer sermon. A translator cuts the time in half but doubles the connection. A longer sermon can be self-translated by the preacher if the preacher is bilingual.

Our solution at the Bible study in Parkmore is to keep the discussion portions in English (the working language of our group) and to make the prayer portions fully multilingual. This is not a complete solution. It is a compromise that respects both the need for shared communication and the importance of multilingual prayer.

### Song Selection

If you have songs in eleven languages, you cannot sing all eleven every meeting. Pick a rotation. Three languages for songs in any given gathering, alternating across the weeks.

Some songs translate well. *"Amazing Grace"* works in every language. *"10,000 Reasons"* has a clear Zulu translation. *"How Great Is Our God"* has been recorded in isiXhosa and seSotho. Many of the contemporary worship songs that started in English have now been translated into the major South African languages. Build a multilingual song library that includes both the international songs and the indigenous South African hymns.

### The Power Dynamics of Language

This is the part that gets least talked about. In a multilingual room, one language is usually dominant. In Johannesburg, that language is usually English. English dominance is not a neutral fact. It shapes who gets to speak, who gets listened to, who gets understood, who gets prayed for.

A truly multilingual Bible study takes active steps to push back on this. It does not assume English is the default. It actively invites prayer in non-English languages. It provides translation when needed. It acknowledges the privilege of English speakers and asks them to use that privilege to make space, not to fill it.

### The Older Generation and the Younger Generation

In many Johannesburg churches, the older generation prefers one or two languages, and the younger generation prefers another. The gap is often between Afrikaans and English, or between isiZulu and English. Multilingual worship can either bridge that gap or widen it, depending on how it is led.

The honest version of this conversation is that some of the older generation feel they have lost their language in the church. They sang in Afrikaans at their mother's church, and now their grandchildren's church sings in English. That is a real loss. The multilingual solution is not to take English away from the grandchildren. It is to give the grandparents back their Afrikaans, and to give the grandchildren a window into it.

## What We Have Learned From Multilingual Bible Study

After ten years of running a Bible study in Johannesburg that has gradually become multilingual, here is what we have learned.

### The Language People Pray In Is the Language They Were Hurt In

This is a hard observation, and not universally true, but it has been true often enough to be worth naming. People who were hurt in a particular language — through a parent, a teacher, a former partner, an apartheid — sometimes find it easier to pray in a different language. The prayer comes from a different part of them. The prayer feels safer.

A multilingual prayer time makes space for this. A monolingual prayer time does not.

### Silence Is a Language Too

When we invite people to pray in their own language, we sometimes get silence. The room goes quiet. The silence is not awkward. It is the silence of people being asked to do something they have not been asked to do before. Wait through it. The prayer comes.

### Translation Is an Act of Love

If you are the only isiZulu speaker in the room, and you pray in isiZulu, and someone at the end of the prayer turns to the person next to them and quietly translates the gist of what you said, that is an act of love. The translation is not just information. It is the room saying *"we wanted to make sure everyone heard you."*

A Bible study community that learns to do this well has learned something the rest of the country could use.

### Multilingual Worship Is Not a Solution to Racism

This is worth saying. A church that sings in eleven languages is not automatically a non-racist church. Language is one part of identity. It is not the whole. A multilingual Bible study can still be a Bible study where the white members dominate the discussion, where the Black members do the hospitality, where the Indian members are invisible, where the coloured members are exoticised.

Multilingual worship is a start. It is not the end. The work of building a truly inclusive Bible study in Johannesburg is much larger than the work of including multiple languages. But it is a real start, and it is a start most Johannesburg churches have not yet made.

## A Practical Multilingual Bible Study Format

If you lead a Bible study in Johannesburg — in Sandton, in Soweto, in Randburg, in Roodepoort, anywhere — and you want to make your Bible study more multilingual without overhauling everything, here is a format we have used successfully.

**Opening (5 minutes)** — A welcome in English, plus a brief welcome in any other language spoken by someone in the room. The leader does not need to be fluent in the second language. A simple *"sanibonani,"* *"dumelang,"* *"goeie more"* is enough. The room hears that the leader knows there are other languages.

**Reading (5 minutes)** — The passage of the week is read in the language most people will understand. Then it is read a second time in a second language. If multiple people speak other languages, invite them to read a verse in their language.

**Discussion (45 minutes)** — Discussion in the working language of the group, usually English. The leader actively invites contributions from people who have been quiet. If translation is needed for an important point, ask for it.

**Prayer (10 minutes)** — Open prayer time in any language. The leader sets the tone by praying first in the working language, then inviting anyone to pray in any language. The room holds the silence while the prayer is happening. After the prayer, anyone who wants can briefly share what they understood, if anything.

**Closing (5 minutes)** — A short blessing in the working language, plus a one-line blessing in any second language spoken in the room.

This format takes about seventy minutes. It is not perfect. It is multilingual in a way that honours the room.

## A Prayer for a Multilingual Church

> *God of every language,*
>
> *You spoke the world into being in a single Word. You confused the languages at Babel. You reunited them at Pentecost. You promise that one day, every nation, tribe, people, and language will stand before your throne.*
>
> *Forgive us for the times we have made our language a wall. Forgive us for the times we have asked others to forget their mother tongue so we could be comfortable. Forgive us for the times we have let English dominate, or Afrikaans dominate, or any language dominate, in a room you made multilingual.*
>
> *Teach us to listen. Teach us to wait through the silence. Teach us to honour the prayer we cannot understand. Teach us to translate not just the words but the heart behind them.*
>
> *Make us a Bible study community where every language is welcomed. Where every prayer is heard. Where every voice has room.*
>
> *We pray in the name of the Word who became flesh and dwelt among us, in a country with many languages and one God.*
> *Amen.*
