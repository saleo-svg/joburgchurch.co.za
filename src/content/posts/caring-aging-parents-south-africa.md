## The Distance That South Africans Carry

There is a particular kind of distance that many South Africans carry. It is the distance between the place where the children have made their lives and the place where the parents are growing old. The distance is sometimes two suburbs. The distance is sometimes two cities. The distance is sometimes two countries. The distance is sometimes two continents.

The distance is not the distance of a holiday. The distance is the distance of the everyday. The distance of the Sunday phone call. The distance of the doctor's appointment you cannot drive to. The distance of the emergency that happens at 2am. The distance of the grandchild who sees the grandparent twice a year.

This post is about how to care for aging parents from a distance. It is not a complete guide. It is not the last word. It is a set of practices that have worked for several people in our Wednesday Bible study community in Sandton, and that have a strong grounding in the Bible.

## What the Bible Says About Caring for Parents

The Bible has a lot to say about this. More than you might expect.

**Exodus 20:12** — *Honour your father and your mother, so that you may live long in the land the Lord your God is giving you.*

**Proverbs 23:22** — *Listen to your father, who gave you life, and do not despise your mother when she is old.*

**1 Timothy 5:4** — *But if a widow has children or grandchildren, these should learn first of all to put their religion into practice by caring for their own family and so repaying their parents and grandparents, for this is pleasing to God.*

**Mark 7:10-13** — *For Moses said, 'Honour your father and mother,' and, 'Anyone who curses their father or mother is to be put to death.' But you say that if anyone declares that what might have been used to help their father or mother is Corban (that is, devoted to God) — then you no longer let them do anything for their father or mother. Thus you nullify the word of God by your tradition you have handed down.*

**Isaiah 46:4** — *Even to your old age and grey hairs I am he, I am he who will sustain you. I have made you and I will carry you; I will sustain you and I will rescue you.*

What you notice when you read these verses together is that caring for parents is not presented as optional. It is presented as a central part of the Christian life. The prodigal son parable is, in many ways, the parable of the bad son. But the older brother in the same parable, the son who stayed home, is also presented as a son who did not understand the heart of the father. Both sons had a problem with the father. The Christian life is the one where the children learn to care for the parents, whether the children are near or far.

## The Five Truths About Long-Distance Care

These five truths have come up so often in our Bible study conversations that we now share them as a list.

### Truth 1 — Care Is Not the Same as Presence

This is the first truth that many of us have to learn. Care is not the same as presence. The parent who lives in Durban and the parent who lives in London can both be cared for. The care looks different. The care is not the same. But the care can be real.

The parent who lives in Durban gets the weekly phone call. The parent who lives in London gets the WhatsApp video call every Sunday. The parent who lives in Cape Town gets the visit twice a year. The parent who lives in the village in Limpopo gets the monthly grocery delivery and the quarterly visit from the cousin.

Care is not the same as presence. Care is the act of paying attention to the parent's life. Care is the act of asking the question. Care is the act of listening to the answer. Care is the act of doing the small thing that the parent needed done this week.

### Truth 2 — The Phone Call Is Not Optional

The phone call is the most basic unit of long-distance care. It is also the most neglected.

The phone call is not the same as the visit. The phone call is not the same as the doctor's appointment. But the phone call is the thing that keeps the relationship alive when the relationship cannot be in the same room.

Many of the people in our Bible study have made a practice of calling the parent at the same time every week. Sunday at 4pm. Wednesday at 7pm. Friday at 9am. The time matters less than the regularity. The parent knows that the phone will ring at the same time. The parent plans the day around the phone call. The parent keeps the phone charged.

The phone call is, in a small way, the rope that ties the family together across the distance.

### Truth 3 — The Local Person Matters More Than the Long-Distance Person

This is the truth that many of the long-distance children have to learn the hard way. The local person matters more than the long-distance person.

The child who lives in Johannesburg and the parent who lives in George cannot be the primary daily companion. The local sibling, the local cousin, the local friend, the local church member — the local person is the one who notices the change in the parent's memory. The local person is the one who notices the empty fridge. The local person is the one who notices the unpaid bill. The local person is the one who takes the parent to the doctor.

The long-distance child cannot replace the local person. The long-distance child can support the local person. The long-distance child can pay the local person. The long-distance child can call the local person to ask how the parent is doing. The long-distance child can fly down when the local person needs a break.

This is the wisdom of the early church in Acts. The early church had a system for caring for widows. The system was not the long-distance deacon calling the widow once a week. The system was the local community taking responsibility for the widow. The long-distance child fits into the local system. The long-distance child does not replace the local system.

### Truth 4 — The Money Conversation Is the Hardest Conversation

The money conversation is the hardest conversation of the long-distance care. The money conversation is the conversation that many people avoid until it is too late.

The money conversation is the conversation about who is paying for the parent's medical aid. The money conversation is the conversation about who is paying for the parent's frail care. The money conversation is the conversation about the parent's will. The money conversation is the conversation about the parent's funeral policy.

The Bible is not silent about the money conversation. The Bible says in 1 Timothy 5:8 — *Anyone who does not provide for their relatives, and especially for their own household, has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever.*

The money conversation is not about being a good child. The money conversation is about being a faithful steward. The money conversation is about using the resources God has given you to care for the people God has given you.

The money conversation is best had before the crisis. The money conversation is best had in the kitchen, not in the hospital waiting room. The money conversation is best had when the parent is still well, not when the parent is in the ICU.

### Truth 5 — The Grief Starts Before the Parent Has Died

This is the truth that no one tells you. The grief starts before the parent has died.

The grief starts the first time the parent forgets your name. The grief starts the first time the parent cannot drive. The grief starts the first time the parent needs help with the bath. The grief starts the first time the parent says something that does not make sense.

The grief is real. The grief needs to be named. The grief needs to be prayed through. The grief needs to be shared with the community.

The Bible calls this kind of grief *lament*. The Psalms are full of lament. The book of Lamentations is full of lament. The grief is not a sign of weak faith. The grief is a sign of honest faith. The grief is the act of bringing the loss to God before the loss has been completed.

## A Practical Long-Distance Care Plan for South Africans

Here is a plan that several of us in the Bible study have adopted. It is not a perfect plan. It is a starting plan.

### Monthly

- Call the parent at the same time every week. The same time. Every week.
- Send a small gift. A doek. A bar of soap. A book. A photo of the grandchild.
- Ask the parent a specific question. Not *how are you*. Ask *what did you eat for breakfast*. Ask *who came to visit this week*. Ask *did you see the doctor*.

### Quarterly

- Visit the parent in person. Even if the visit is short. Even if the visit is expensive.
- Take the parent to a doctor's appointment. The visit is not just for the social call. The visit is for the medical check.
- Sit with the local person who supports the parent. Have coffee. Ask what they need. Pay for it if you can.

### Annually

- Have the money conversation. Talk about the will. Talk about the funeral policy. Talk about the medical aid. Talk about the long-term care plan.
- Update the emergency plan. Who is called first if the parent is in the hospital. Who is the contact for the medical aid. Who is the contact for the bank.
- Consider spending a longer stretch with the parent. A two-week visit. A month. The long stretch is different from the short visit. The long stretch is the one where you see the daily life.

### When the Crisis Comes

- Get on the first flight. Get on the first bus. Get on the first train. The crisis is not the time to be polite about the obligations.
- Call the church community. Ask for prayer. Ask for help. The community is the one who will hold the family together in the long months of the recovery.
- Take care of yourself. The parent needs you to be well. The parent needs you to be sleeping. The parent needs you to be eating. You cannot give care from an empty cup.

## A Prayer for the Distance

If you are carrying the distance this week — the distance between you and the parent in Cape Town, the distance between you and the parent in the village in Limpopo, the distance between you and the parent in the home in Pretoria — here is a prayer.

> *God of the long distance,*
>
> *We carry the weight of the distance. The distance between the place where we work and the place where our parents are growing old. The distance between the life we have made and the life we came from.*
>
> *Help us to call. Help us to visit. Help us to ask the questions that need to be asked. Help us to have the conversations that need to be had.*
>
> *Help us to support the local person who is doing the daily work. Help us to pay for the help when the help is needed. Help us to be present even when we cannot be in the room.*
>
> *We pray for our parents. We pray for the parent who is in the home. We pray for the parent who is still in the house. We pray for the parent whose mind is still clear. We pray for the parent whose mind is leaving.*
>
> *We thank you for the years you gave us with them. We thank you for the prayers they prayed for us. We thank you for the rooibos tea and the Sunday lunch and the long phone calls and the visits to the school.*
>
> *Sustain us as we sustain them. Carry us as we carry them. Hold us as we hold them.*
>
> *In the name of the one who, on the cross, asked you to care for his mother.*
> *Amen.*
