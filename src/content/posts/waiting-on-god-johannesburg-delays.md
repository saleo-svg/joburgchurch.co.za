## The City That Teaches You to Wait

If you have lived in Johannesburg for more than a year, you have learned to wait. You wait at the traffic light on William Nicol for three cycles. You wait in the Home Affairs queue in Cresta for the entire morning. You wait for Eskom to bring the power back. You wait for the recruiter to call back. You wait for the visa to clear. You wait for the hospital appointment. You wait for the builder, the plumber, the car mechanic, the new washing machine part, the test result, the promotion decision, the apology.

Johannesburg is, in many ways, a city of long pauses. The pauses are not always bad. The pauses are not always wasted. But the pauses are real.

This post is about what to do with the pauses. It is about what the Bible says about waiting, and how the people in our Wednesday Bible study community have learned to wait well.

## What the Bible Actually Says About Waiting

The Bible has a lot to say about waiting. More than you might expect.

**Psalm 27:14** — *Wait for the Lord; be strong and take heart and wait for the Lord.*

**Psalm 130:5** — *I wait for the Lord, my whole being waits, and in his word I put my hope.*

**Lamentations 3:25-26** — *The Lord is good to those whose hope is in him, to the one who seeks him; it is good to wait quietly for the salvation of the Lord.*

**Isaiah 40:31** — *But those who hope in the Lord will renew their strength. They will soar on wings like eagles; they will run and not grow weary, they will walk and not be faint.*

**Habakkuk 2:3** — *For the revelation is awaiting an appointed time; it speaks of the end and will not prove false. Though it linger, wait for it; it will certainly come and will not delay.*

What you notice when you read these verses together is that waiting is not presented as a passive thing. Waiting is not the same as doing nothing. Waiting, in the Bible, is an active posture. It is the act of holding onto something you cannot yet see. It is the act of trusting that the next thing is coming, even when the next thing has not yet arrived.

This is the Joburg kind of waiting. The Joburg kind of waiting is not the waiting of a person who has nowhere to be. It is the waiting of a person who has a place to be, and who is currently stuck between where they are and where they need to be.

## Why Waiting Is Harder in Johannesburg Specifically

Three reasons waiting is harder in Johannesburg than in most other cities.

### 1. The Delays Are Longer

In many cities, the delays are short. A traffic light is ninety seconds. A queue is twenty minutes. A visa is six weeks. In Johannesburg, the delays are often much longer. A traffic light on a Friday afternoon can be forty-five minutes. A queue at the licensing department in Johannesburg central can be the entire day. A visa can be four months. A waiting list for a specialist can be six months. A power outage can be four hours at a time, eight hours a day, every day for a week.

The Joburg pauses are not the small pauses of a busy life. They are the long pauses of a life that has been paused, and you do not know when it will be unpaused.

### 2. The Delays Are Often Unpredictable

In some cities, the delays are predictable. You can plan around them. In Johannesburg, the delays are often unpredictable. The Eskom schedule is published but is not always followed. The visa appointment is confirmed but is sometimes cancelled. The Uber is two minutes away but can be twenty. The recruiter says one week but actually means six weeks.

The unprediability is the hard part. When you cannot predict the delay, you cannot plan around the delay. You have to learn to live inside the delay.

### 3. The Delays Are Often Quiet

In some cities, there is something to do while you wait. There is a coffee shop. There is a park. There is a conversation. In Johannesburg, the waits are often quiet. You sit in a queue and you sit in a car and you sit in a hospital waiting room and there is nothing to do but wait.

It is in the quiet that the faith questions come. *Why is this taking so long? What is God doing? Is God doing anything? Should I be doing something else?*

## How the Sandton Bible Study Community Has Learned to Wait

Over the last three years, several members of our Wednesday Bible study community have shared the strategies they have developed for waiting well. These are not theological theories. They are practices that have been tested in real Jozi situations.

### Practice 1 — Pray for the Wait, Not Just the Resolution

When you find yourself in a long pause, the first instinct is to pray for the resolution. *God, please let me get the job. God, please let the visa clear. God, please let the power come back.*

There is nothing wrong with that prayer. But the people who have learned to wait well in Johannesburg have learned to pray for the wait itself. *God, please help me in this pause. Please give me something to do in this pause. Please help me not to waste this pause.*

This is the prayer of David in the Psalms. He prayed for the resolution, but he also prayed for the wait. *How long, O Lord? Will you forget me forever?* (Psalm 13:1). He did not pretend the wait was easy. He did not pretend the wait was short. He named the wait, and he prayed in the wait.

### Practice 2 — Use the Wait for Something You Could Not Have Done in the Busy Time

One of the most unexpectedly useful things about a long pause is that it creates space for the things the busy life did not have space for.

A home affairs queue of four hours is, in some ways, the only time in the week when you will sit still for four hours. The same applies to a hospital waiting room. The same applies to a delay at the airport. The same applies to a load-shedding evening.

The people who have learned to wait well in Johannesburg have learned to use the wait for the things the busy time did not allow. Read the passage for Wednesday Bible study. Read the book of Psalms slowly. Pray through the prayer list. Sit with a friend who is also waiting. Take a walk around the block. Look at the jacarandas. Listen to the rain.

The wait becomes, in a small way, a gift. Not because the wait is good. The wait is often bad. But because the wait can be used.

### Practice 3 — Do Not Make the Major Decision in the Pause

This is the one most people wish they had heard before they made a bad decision.

When you are in a long pause — a job loss, a relationship ending, a visa difficulty, a medical waiting period — the temptation is to make a major decision in the pause. The pause feels unbearable. The decision feels urgent. The decision feels like the only way out of the pause.

The people who have learned to wait well in Johannesburg have learned to wait until the pause is over before they make the major decision. The job decision can wait two weeks. The relationship decision can wait two months. The house decision can wait three months. The career change can wait six months.

This is the wisdom of Proverbs 19:2 — *Desire without knowledge is not good — how much more will hasty feet miss the way!*

### Practice 4 — Keep Showing Up

The hardest part of a long pause is the showing up. The Bible study on Wednesday night. The gym on Tuesday morning. The phone call to the friend you promised to call. The application you submitted and have not heard back about.

The people who have learned to wait well in Johannesburg have learned to keep showing up. Not because the showing up is going to fix the pause. The showing up is not going to fix the pause. But the showing up is going to keep you in the life. The showing up is going to keep you connected to the people. The showing up is going to keep you holding onto the thing you are waiting for.

Showing up is an act of faith. It is the act of saying, *I am still here. I am still waiting. I am still hoping.*

## A Prayer for the Long Joburg Pause

If you are in a long pause this week — a visa, a job, a test result, a power outage that has been going for three days, a Home Affairs queue that has eaten your entire Saturday — here is a prayer. You can say it in the queue. You can say it in the car. You can say it with the candles on.

> *God of the long pause,*
>
> *We are tired. We have been in this pause for a long time. The visa. The job. The test result. The power. The queue. The appointment. The phone call that has not yet come.*
>
> *We do not pretend the wait is easy. We name it as hard. We name it as exhausting. We name it as the kind of wait that makes us wonder if we are still in the right place.*
>
> *But we ask you to be with us in the pause. We ask you to do something in the pause that we cannot do in the busy time. We ask you to give us the grace to keep showing up. To keep praying. To keep reading the passage. To keep calling the friend.*
>
> *We trust that the pause is not the end of the story. We trust that you are doing something in the pause that we cannot yet see. We trust that the next thing is coming, even when the next thing has not yet arrived.*
>
> *Give us the strength of those who wait for you. Give us the patience of the psalmists. Give us the hope of the prophets.*
>
> *In the name of the one who waited in the wilderness for forty days and forty nights, and who rose again on the third day.*
> *Amen.*

## A Note on the Long Pause and the Bible Study

One of the quiet things that has happened in our Wednesday Bible study over the last few years is that the long pauses have started to come up in the discussions. Someone shares a visa that has been pending for six months. Someone shares a job application that has been pending for four months. Someone shares a medical test that has been pending for two months. Someone shares a relationship that has been pending for a year.

What we have learned is that the long pauses are not a side topic of the Christian life in Johannesburg. The long pauses are the Christian life in Johannesburg. The Bible is, in many ways, a book about waiting. The Israelites waited in the wilderness. David waited in the cave. The exiles waited in Babylon. The early church waited for the second coming. The whole arc of the Bible is the long pause between the first promise and the final fulfilment.

The Wednesday Bible study has become, in a small way, a place where the long pauses are named. They are not fixed. They are not always resolved quickly. But they are named. And the naming is the first step to the resolution.
