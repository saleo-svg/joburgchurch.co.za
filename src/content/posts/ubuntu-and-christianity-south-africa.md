## The Word That Took Two Centuries to Reach the Rest of the World

If you grew up in South Africa, you have heard the word *Ubuntu* since before you can remember. If you grew up anywhere else, you probably first heard it in 1999, when Desmond Tutu used it to describe the spirit of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission. Since then, it has travelled.

The short version of Ubuntu is: *"Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu"* — a person is a person through other people. The shorthand version, the one that gets printed on conference slides in Brussels and New York, is: *"I am because we are."*

The version most South Africans actually grew up with is simpler and harder. It is the voice of your mother when you came home from school complaining about the child who had no lunch: *"Wena, that child is you. Give them half of yours."*

It is the principle at the heart of how Ubuntu meets Christianity — and it is the principle that, in our experience, the Bible study communities of Johannesburg are still trying to live out.

## What Ubuntu Actually Means in Practice

Ubuntu is often translated badly. The English shorthand — *I am because we are* — sounds like a philosophical slogan. In Zulu and Xhosa households, it is more practical than that. It shows up in specific behaviours.

Ubuntu is the neighbour who leaves a plate of *pap and morogo* on your stoep without knocking, because they noticed your lights were off at the usual dinner time.

Ubuntu is the grandmother in Soweto who walks two streets over to check that the new mother has someone with her, because the first forty days after birth are not something a woman should do alone.

Ubuntu is the uncle in Alexandra who pays school fees for a child that is not his, because that child's mother worked with his sister in 1994 and he has not forgotten.

Ubuntu is the way the queue at the *spaza shop* quietly reorganises itself so the elderly person does not have to stand for forty minutes.

These are not metaphors. They are the lived grammar of Ubuntu, and they are the grammar in which most South Africans — including most of the people in our Wednesday Bible study in Parkmore — already operate long before they ever open a Bible.

## Where the Bible Has Been Saying This for Three Thousand Years

The interesting thing, when you start comparing Ubuntu to Scripture, is how much overlap there is. Ubuntu did not arrive with the missionaries. It was already the cultural ground into which Christianity was planted, and it shaped how South African Christianity took root.

Here are five biblical passages that, when read next to Ubuntu, sound less foreign and more like home.

### Leviticus 19:18 — Love Your Neighbour as Yourself

Ubuntu and this verse are sometimes mistaken for opposites. They are not. The verse from Leviticus is often read in English as an instruction to the individual: love your neighbour, treat them well, do not steal from them. In a culture that already believed a person is a person through other people, this verse read very differently. It was an instruction to the community: the *self* in *"as yourself"* is not the individual self. It is the communal self. You cannot love your neighbour *as yourself* if your sense of self already includes your neighbour.

This is the verse Ubuntu had been practising for generations.

### Acts 2:42-47 — The Early Church Devoted Themselves to Fellowship

Read slowly. *"All the believers were together and had everything in common. They sold property and possessions to give to anyone who had need."* That is not a description of an early Christian commune in Jerusalem. That is a description of Ubuntu in its purest form. The early church did not invent mutual care. They received it from a Jewish tradition that already had it, and they preached it in a Greco-Roman world that did not.

### 1 Corinthians 12:12-27 — The Body of Christ

The body metaphor is Paul's most famous. It is also the most Ubuntu passage in the New Testament. *"If one part suffers, every part suffers with it; if one part is honoured, every part rejoices with it."* That is *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*, translated into Greek by a Jewish man writing to a Roman audience. The Corinthians had never heard of Ubuntu. They knew exactly what Paul meant anyway.

### Philippians 2:1-4 — Consider Others Better Than Yourselves

The King James Version of this passage is well known. The Message paraphrase is, in our opinion, closer to the Ubuntu spirit: *"Don't push your way to the front. Don't sweet-talk your way to the top. Put yourself aside, and help others get ahead."* That is Ubuntu in Christian dress. The humility Paul is describing is not low self-esteem. It is a strong sense of self held lightly enough that other people's needs can move in.

### Galatians 6:2 — Carry Each Other's Burdens

*"Carry each other's burdens, and in this way you will fulfil the law of Christ."* This is the verse that, in our Wednesday Bible study in Johannesburg, has come up most often over the last ten years. It is short enough to memorise. It is hard enough to live out. And it is the verse that, for many South African Christians, finally gives them permission to do what their grandmothers already taught them.

## Where Ubuntu and Christianity Diverge

The honest version of this conversation has to include the places where Ubuntu and Christian theology do not line up cleanly. There are at least three.

### The Individual and the Community

Ubuntu tends to dissolve the individual into the community. Christianity, while deeply communal, insists on the irreducible worth of the individual. Every person is made in the image of God. Every person is the object of Christ's atoning work. The Christian tradition has, in some of its worst moments, used that conviction to support terrible things — including apartheid. Ubuntu has its own shadow side here, when the community pressure to conform overrides the call to protect the vulnerable.

Both traditions need each other. Ubuntu without the individual becomes a pressure cooker. Christianity without the community becomes a private faith with no muscle.

### Ancestors

In many South African Christian communities, the relationship with the ancestors is one of the most contested theological questions. Ubuntu, in its traditional form, treats the ancestors as part of the ongoing community — the *ubuntu* does not stop at death. Christianity, in most of its Protestant forms, treats veneration of the ancestors as incompatible with worship of God alone. Catholic and Anglican traditions tend to take a softer line, seeing the communion of saints as a parallel concept.

This is a real disagreement. It deserves honest conversation, not a quick answer. We have spent Wednesday evenings in Parkmore on this exact question. There is no verse that settles it in five minutes. There is, however, a way to talk about it that respects both traditions.

### The Source of the Moral Claim

Ubuntu ultimately grounds its moral claim in the community. Christianity grounds its moral claim in God. This difference matters. When the community is wrong — as South African communities have been wrong, repeatedly — Ubuntu alone does not have a vocabulary to name that. When the Christian tradition has been wrong, it has usually had to be corrected by other Christians, often by the very communities it had marginalised.

## What This Means for a Bible Study in Johannesburg

A Bible study in Johannesburg that takes Ubuntu seriously will look different from one that does not. Here is what we have noticed in our ten years of running a Wednesday evening Bible study in Parkmore.

### It Will Be More Welcoming

The default culture of a small Bible study is often individualist. You come, you study, you go home, you come back next week. Ubuntu pushes against that default. In a community shaped by Ubuntu, you notice when someone is absent. You ask. You follow up. You bring food. You include the new person not because the welcome committee is doing its job, but because the absence of a new person would itself be noticed.

### It Will Be Less Hierarchical

Ubuntu has a healthy suspicion of hierarchy that goes too far. So does Jesus. The disciples were arguing about who was the greatest. Jesus put a child in the middle of them. The Ubuntu instinct — that the leader is the one who serves — is the same instinct. In a Bible study, this means the leader is not the one with the most answers. The leader is the one who keeps the room safe enough for the people who are afraid to ask.

### It Will Be More Honest About Suffering

Ubuntu does not pretend suffering is not real. It names it, grieves it, and insists on staying present with the person who is going through it. The South African Bible study tradition sometimes inherits a Western Christianity that prefers the triumphant answer. Ubuntu pushes back on that. There are Psalms that have no resolution. There is a book of Job that refuses its main character a satisfying answer. Ubuntu reads these passages and recognises them.

### It Will Be More Politically Engaged

Ubuntu is not a private spirituality. It has always been public. So is the Bible. A Bible study in Johannesburg that is shaped by both will not be able to ignore the unjust systems around it. It will notice when load-shedding hits a particular neighbourhood harder than another. It will notice when a community class is only accessible to people who can drive. It will ask, and it will act.

## A Practice for Your Own Bible Study

If you lead or attend a Bible study anywhere in Johannesburg — or anywhere in South Africa, or anywhere in the world — here is a practice we recommend. Once a month, before you open the Bible, ask one question out loud:

*"Who in our community is not in the room tonight, and what would it take for them to be here?"*

That is Ubuntu applied to Bible study. That is the question that, in our experience, has done more to shape our Wednesday evenings in Parkmore than any sermon or curriculum.
