## The Unique Situation of the South African Christian Parent

There is a situation that is so common in South Africa that it is rarely talked about. It is the situation of the Christian parent who speaks one language at home and another language at the school gate. The parent who reads the bedtime story in Sepedi while the child does the homework in English. The parent who prays in isiXhosa while the Sunday school uses English. The parent who knows the proverb in isiZulu but has to translate it for the child because the child's world has moved to English.

This situation is not the exception in South Africa. This situation is the rule. South Africa has eleven official languages. Most Christian households in Johannesburg operate in two of them. The Bible study that meets on a Wednesday evening in Sandton includes parents from Setswana-speaking homes, isiZulu-speaking homes, isiXhosa-speaking homes, Afrikaans-speaking homes, Sesotho-speaking homes, and English-speaking homes. The Christian parenting question that every one of these parents carries is the same question. *How do I pass my faith to my child when the language of the faith at home is not the language my child lives in during the week?*

This post is for that parent.

## What the Bible Actually Says About Passing Faith to Children

The Bible is unusually clear that the passing of faith to the next generation is one of the central tasks of the Christian life. It is not an optional extra. It is one of the main things.

**Deuteronomy 6:6-7** — *These commandments that I give you today are to be on your hearts. Impress them on your children. Talk about them when you sit at home and when you walk along the road, when you lie down and when you get up.*

**Psalm 78:4-7** — *We will not hide them from their descendants; we will tell the next generation the praiseworthy deeds of the Lord, his power, and the wonders he has done. He decreed statutes for Jacob and established the law in Israel, which he commanded our ancestors to teach their children, so the next generation would know them, even the children yet to be born, and they in turn would tell their children.*

**Proverbs 22:6** — *Start children off on the way they should go, and even when they are old they will not turn from it.*

**2 Timothy 3:14-15** — *But as for you, continue in what you have learned and have become convinced of, because you know those from whom you learned it, and how from infancy you have known the holy Scriptures, which are able to make you wise for salvation through faith in Christ Jesus.*

**Ephesians 6:4** — *Fathers, do not exasperate your children; instead, bring them up in the training and instruction of the Lord.*

What you notice in these verses is that the passing of faith is presented as something that happens in the everyday. Not in the Sunday school lesson. Not in the special church event. In the *sitting at home and walking along the road and lying down and getting up*. In the *telling the next generation*. In the *training and instruction of the Lord*. The everyday. The kitchen table. The car ride. The bedtime. The homework time. The language that is actually being used at that moment.

This is the verse that the multilingual South African Christian parent has to read carefully. The verse assumes that the faith will be passed in the language the family actually uses. The verse does not require the parent to translate the faith into the language the school uses. The verse does require the parent to pass the faith in the language the home uses.

## The Three Temptations of the Multilingual South African Christian Parent

### Temptation 1 — To Switch to English at Home

Many Christian parents, after a few years of school, switch to English at home. The switch is usually made for the child's sake — to help with the schoolwork, to build the vocabulary, to prepare for the world the child is growing into. The switch is rarely a rejection of the home language. The switch is usually a quiet decision that the home language is no longer useful.

The Bible does not support this switch. The Bible assumes the home is the primary place of faith formation. The home is the primary place because the home is where the *sitting at home and walking along the road* happens. The home is where the bedtime prayer happens. The home is where the bedtime story is told. The home is where the *talking about them when you sit at home* happens. If the home language is the language of the bedtime prayer, then the home language is also the language of the faith.

### Temptation 2 — To Make the Faith English-Only

The opposite temptation is to make the faith an English-only thing. The English Bible on the shelf. The English worship songs on the speaker. The English prayer at the table. The home language is for the everyday. The English is for the faith.

The Bible does not support this either. The Bible says in Psalm 22:3 that God inhabits the praises of his people. The Bible says in 1 Corinthians 14:15 that the Christian should pray with the spirit and pray with the mind. The Bible assumes that the prayer rises up in whatever language the heart is using. If the heart is using Setswana, the prayer will rise up in Setswana. If the heart is using isiZulu, the prayer will rise up in isiZulu. The faith is not the English-only faith. The faith is the language of the heart.

### Temptation 3 — To Leave the Faith-Forming to the Church

Many Christian parents leave the faith-forming to the church. The church teaches the Bible. The Sunday school teaches the Bible stories. The youth ministry teaches the doctrine. The home provides the transport.

The Bible does not support this either. The Deuteronomy 6 passage assumes that the home is the primary place of faith formation. The Psalm 78 passage assumes that each generation tells the next. The Ephesians 6 passage assumes that the parent is the primary discipler of the child. The church supports the home. The church does not replace the home. The Sunday school reinforces what the parent has been teaching all week. The Sunday school is not the primary place.

## What the Multilingual South African Christian Home Looks Like in Practice

These are the practices that several families in our Wednesday Bible study community in Sandton have developed. They are not theoretical. They have been tested across isiZulu-English homes, isiXhosa-English homes, Afrikaans-English homes, Setswana-English homes, Sesotho-English homes, and Shona-English homes.

### Practice 1 — The Home Language Is the Prayer Language

The bedtime prayer is prayed in the home language. The meal prayer is prayed in the home language. The Sunday morning prayer is prayed in the home language. The car prayer (when there has been a near miss on William Nicol) is prayed in the home language. The home language is the prayer language because the home language is the language of the heart. The child may grow up to pray in English at a church prayer meeting. The child will always pray in the home language when no one is watching.

### Practice 2 — The Bible Story Is Told in the Home Language First

The Bible story is told at the kitchen table in the home language first. The story of David and Goliath is told in Setswana on the rug after bath time. The story of the Prodigal Son is told in isiXhosa on the drive to school. The story of Joseph is told in Afrikaans at the dinner table. The English Bible comes later. The English Bible is for the school and the church. The home language Bible is for the kitchen table.

Many of the parents in our Bible study have made a small collection of Bible stories in their home language. The Parents-in-Trust Bible Story Book in isiZulu. The Bible in Sepedi for Children. The Afrikaans Kinderbybel. The Setswana Bible Stories. The child grows up hearing the Bible in two languages, and the faith is anchored in both.

### Practice 3 — The Worship Song Is in the Home Language

The worship song on a Saturday morning is in the home language. The parent puts on the hymn in isiZulu from the township church the parent grew up in. The parent puts on the hymn in Setswana from the grandmother's house. The parent puts on the Afrikaans hymn from the school assembly. The English worship song is added as the child grows older. The home language worship song is the foundation.

Several of the families in our Bible study have made a playlist of hymns in their home language. The playlist is played on Saturday mornings while the house is being cleaned. The playlist is played on Sunday afternoons while the lunch is being prepared. The child grows up with the hymns in the home language as the soundtrack of the home.

### Practice 4 — The Grandparent Is the Faith-Forming Helper

The grandparent is the secret weapon of the multilingual Christian home in South Africa. The grandparent is the one who speaks the home language without the English mixed in. The grandparent is the one who tells the Bible story in the home language without translating it. The grandparent is the one who prays in the home language at the right pace. The grandparent is the one who sings the hymn in the home language without looking at the words.

Many of the families in our Bible study have made a deliberate decision to send the child to the grandparent for a week in the school holiday. The week is not just for the spoiling. The week is for the language. The week is for the hymns. The week is for the Bible stories. The week is for the grandmother's prayer. The grandparent is the helper of the faith formation. The grandparent is the one the Deuteronomy 6 vision assumed.

### Practice 5 — The Two Bibles on the Shelf

Many of the families in our Bible study have two Bibles on the shelf. The English Bible (probably the NIV or the ESV) is for the church, the school, the Bible study. The home language Bible is for the kitchen table, the bedtime, the car ride. The two Bibles are not in competition. The two Bibles are for different moments.

When the child is old enough, the parent gives the child both Bibles. The child learns that the Bible is not a single book in a single language. The child learns that the Bible is the word of God in whatever language the heart can receive it.

## The Two Questions Every Multilingual South African Christian Parent Asks

### Question 1 — "Will My Child's Faith Survive the English-Only School?"

Yes. The faith will survive the English-only school. The faith has survived the English-only school in every generation of South African Christians since 1652. The faith survives because the faith is not a language. The faith is a person. The person is Jesus. The person is the same in English and in isiZulu and in Setswana. The parent who has anchored the faith in the home language has given the child a foundation that the English-only school cannot shake. The parent who has not anchored the faith in any language has given the child a faith that the English-only school will erode.

The parent does not need to choose between English and the home language for the child's future. The parent needs to choose the home language for the child's faith. The English will come at school. The faith has to come at home.

### Question 2 — "What If My Home Language Is Not the Language of My Heart?"

Some South African Christian parents grew up in English. Some grew up in Afrikaans. Some grew up in a home where the parents deliberately did not pass on their own home language because of the pain of apartheid. Some grew up in a home where the language was lost across one or two generations. The home language is not always the language of the parent's heart. The home language is sometimes a reconstructed thing. The home language is sometimes a deliberate choice the parent is making now.

The Bible does not require the parent to use the language of the grandparent. The Bible requires the parent to pass the faith in the language of the home. If the language of the home is English, the faith is passed in English. If the language of the home is a reconstructed language the parent is learning alongside the child, the faith is passed in the reconstructing. The Bible assumes the language of the home. The Bible does not assume any particular language.

## A Prayer for the Multilingual South African Christian Parent

If you are a parent in Johannesburg, in Sandton, in Randburg, in Soweto — passing your faith to your child across two languages, three languages, four languages — here is a prayer.

> *God of the home,*
>
> *We thank you for the children you have given us. We thank you for the kitchen table where the Bible story is told. We thank you for the bedtime where the prayer is prayed. We thank you for the car ride where the hymn is sung.*
>
> *We confess that we are afraid that the English of the school will crowd out the language of the home. We confess that we are afraid that the language of the home will not equip the child for the world. We confess that we have tried to choose, and we have chosen badly.*
>
> *Help us to pass the faith in the language of the home. Help us to pray in the language of the home. Help us to sing in the language of the home. Help us to tell the Bible story in the language of the home. Help us to honour the grandparent who carries the language we cannot carry.*
>
> *Give us the patience for the bedtime story told in the same words every night. Give us the patience for the hymn on the playlist for the hundredth time. Give us the patience for the prayer that the child says back to us in their own halting version.*
>
> *We trust that the faith passed in the language of the home will survive the English of the school. We trust that the prayer prayed in the language of the heart will rise up to you. We trust that the word of God in the language of the home will not return empty.*
>
> *In the name of the one who spoke Aramaic in the home, who prayed in Aramaic in the garden, who read the Hebrew scriptures in the synagogue.*
> *Amen.*
