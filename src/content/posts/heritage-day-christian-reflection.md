## The Day South Africa Cooks Together

On 24 September every year, South Africans do something remarkable. We light fires. We stand around them with people we love. We cook meat. We drink something cold. We call it a braai, but it is actually a national ritual of remembering who we are.

Heritage Day used to be called Shaka Day in KwaZulu-Natal. It became a national public holiday in 1996, when the new South African government chose to mark the day not with the triumph of one group but with the recognition of all the cultures that make up the country. The brief is simple: celebrate your heritage, and respect the heritage of the people standing next to you at the braai stand.

Christians in South Africa have always had a slightly complicated relationship with Heritage Day. Some churches lean into it, using the day to celebrate the diversity of the church and the way God has brought different cultures together. Some churches ignore it, treating it as a secular holiday that distracts from the gospel. Some churches — particularly the Afrikaans churches — have a difficult history with the day, given its roots in the post-apartheid reimagining of national identity.

This post is for the Christian who wants to take Heritage Day seriously. It is not a defence of any one position. It is a reflection on what the day could mean for a Bible study community in Johannesburg that is trying to take both the Bible and South Africa seriously.

## What Heritage Day Actually Is

The simplest way to understand Heritage Day is the way most South Africans actually experience it. It is a day to:

- Cook with your family in the way your family cooks
- Wear the clothes your culture wears
- Speak the language your grandmother spoke to you
- Honour the people who came before you

It is also a public holiday, which means most South Africans get the day off. This is, depending on your outlook, either a great gift or an invitation to overthink the day.

The deeper version of Heritage Day — the one that the South African government was trying to surface in 1996 — is an invitation to a national conversation about identity. Who are we? What is the country? Whose story gets told? Whose story gets left out? What does it mean to belong here, and to whom do we belong?

These are not South African questions alone. They are biblical questions. The Bible has been asking them for three thousand years.

## What the Bible Says About Heritage

The Bible has a lot to say about heritage, and most of it is uncomfortable in a useful way.

### Genesis 12:1-3 — The Call to Leave and the Call to Bless

God's call to Abram is one of the foundational calls in the Bible. *"Leave your country, your people and your father's household and go to the land I will show you."* The call is to leave. The call is also to become a blessing: *"all peoples on earth will be blessed through you."*

This is the tension that Heritage Day tries to hold. The call to remember where you came from. The call to become something new with the people around you. The two calls are not opposites. They are the same call. You cannot bless the peoples of the earth if you have not first honoured the people you came from.

A South African Christian reading this passage in 2026 cannot avoid noticing that the country is full of people whose forebears were brought here against their will, and people whose forebears came here as settlers. Both groups have a heritage. Both groups are called to be a blessing. The call has not been answered. The Bible does not pretend it has.

### Deuteronomy 26:1-11 — Bringing the First Fruits

The Israelites are told to bring the first fruits of the harvest to the priest. They are also told to recite their story: *"My father was a wandering Aramean, and he went down into Egypt with a few people and lived there and became a great nation."* The recitation is the ritual. You cannot bring the first fruits without remembering the story.

Heritage Day, when done well, is a recitation. You tell the story. You remember the wandering. You remember the hardship. You remember the becoming. Then you bring the first fruits of who you now are to the table, and you share them with the people around you.

The South African braai is, in its own way, the first fruits. The recipe your grandmother taught you is the offering. The table you set it on is the altar.

### Acts 17:26 — From One, Many

At the Areopagus, Paul tells the Athenian philosophers that God *"made from one man every nation of men to live on all the face of the earth, having determined allotted periods and the boundaries of their habitation."* The Christian tradition has often read this verse as God placing nations where they belong. It can also be read as God honouring the diversity of the nations.

Both readings matter for Heritage Day. The first reading says: every nation is part of God's plan. The second reading says: every nation has a place. A South Africa that takes both readings seriously is a South Africa that knows its own diversity is not a problem to be solved. It is part of the design.

### Revelation 7:9-10 — The Vision of the Multitude

The end of the Bible includes a vision of a great multitude that no one could count, from every nation, tribe, people, and language, standing before the throne. John is writing to seven churches in Asia Minor. He is describing a future that the seven churches, who lived in their own particular places and cultures, will one day be part of.

This is the vision Heritage Day points towards without quite naming it. A gathering that is not the erasure of cultures but the celebration of them. A multitude that is not a single voice but every voice. A South Africa that takes this seriously is a South Africa that knows the goal is not to flatten what is different but to bring what is different into the same room and let it be itself.

## What This Means for a Bible Study in Johannesburg

If you lead or attend a Bible study in Johannesburg — in Sandton, in Soweto, in Randburg, in Roodepoort — Heritage Day is an opportunity. Here are some ways Bible study communities in Johannesburg have used the day.

### A Heritage Day Bible Study on Heritage

Some Bible study communities choose the Sunday closest to 24 September as the day to do a Bible study on the Bible's teaching on heritage. They pick the passages above, or passages of their own. They share their own family's heritage with the room. They pray for South Africa.

This is a low-cost, high-meaning thing to do. It costs nothing except the willingness to be honest about where you come from.

### A Heritage Day Shared Meal

Other Bible study communities use Heritage Day as an excuse for a shared meal. Everyone brings a dish from their heritage. The Zulu member brings *amasi*. The Afrikaans member brings *koesisters*. The Jewish member brings *challah*. The Korean member brings kimchi. The room eats the food of each other's cultures, and the conversation goes where the conversation goes.

We did this in our Saturday evening in Parkmore last Heritage Day. Twelve people. Eight different heritage backgrounds. Forty dishes on the table. The most honest Bible study we had all year.

### A Heritage Day Service Project

Some Bible study communities use Heritage Day to do a service project in a part of Johannesburg they do not usually visit. They go to a community centre in Soweto. They help at a feeding scheme in Hillbrow. They volunteer at a hospice in Houghton. They come home with a different sense of the country.

This is good, with the caveat from the previous post about township Bible study. You are going to serve, but you are also going to learn. The community you visit has resources you do not have. Treat them as partners, not as beneficiaries.

### A Heritage Day Reading List

For Bible study communities that want to mark Heritage Day but do not have the time for a meal or a project, a reading list is a good option. Some books we recommend:

- *No Future Without Forgiveness* by Desmond Tutu — the Archbishop's account of the Truth and Reconciliation Commission, and one of the most important South African books of the last thirty years.
- *Long Walk to Freedom* by Nelson Mandela — the autobiography, still the most accessible account of the long walk to 1994.
- *Born a Crime* by Trevor Noah — the comedian's memoir of growing up during apartheid. Funny, painful, and accurate.
- *The Heart of Redness* by Zakes Mda — a novel that weaves the Xhosa cattle-killing of 1856 into a contemporary South African story.
- *Disgrace* by J.M. Coetzee — the novel that won the Nobel Prize and that still, twenty-six years later, asks the question no one in South Africa has fully answered.
- *The Bible* — still the best book on heritage we have found.

## A Heritage Day Prayer for South Africa

If you are part of a Bible study in Johannesburg, here is a prayer you could use on Heritage Day, or any day you are thinking about what it means to be a Christian in this country.

> *God of Abraham and Sarah, God of Moses and Miriam, God of David and Ruth, God of Jesus and Mary Magdalene —*
>
> *We thank you for the country you have given us. We thank you for its beauty. We thank you for its complexity. We thank you for the people you have placed in it.*
>
> *We confess the ways we have failed each other. The ways we have spoken over each other. The ways we have made our heritage a wall instead of a door. We confess the times we have asked our brothers and sisters to forget where they come from, instead of asking them to tell us about it.*
>
> *On this Heritage Day, give us the courage to remember. The courage to share. The courage to listen. The courage to sit at the same table with people whose stories we do not know, and to eat the food they have brought, and to be changed by it.*
>
> *Make us a country that is not afraid of its past. Make us a country that is not ashamed of its future. Make us a country where every heritage is honoured, every language is heard, and every person is welcomed.*
>
> *We pray in the name of the one who broke down the dividing wall.*
> *Amen.*

## The Braai That Is Also a Prayer

There is a tradition in some South African churches of blessing the braai on Heritage Day. The minister walks around the fire, prays over the meat, prays over the people, and sends everyone home with full bellies and a sense that the day has been marked properly.

We love this tradition. It is also, we think, a metaphor for what Heritage Day is for the Christian. The braai is the gathering. The fire is the Holy Spirit. The meat is the offering each of us brings from our own heritage. The blessing is the work of God in the middle of it.

This Heritage Day, light a fire. Cook something from your heritage. Invite someone whose heritage is different from yours. Eat together. Talk about where you come from. Pray for the country.

It is one of the most South African things a Christian can do.
