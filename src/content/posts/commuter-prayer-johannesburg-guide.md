## The Train That Started a Prayer Habit

The first time I prayed out loud on the Gautrain, I was sitting in the quiet coach between Rosebank and Sandton. It was a Tuesday morning in 2022. I had a 7:30am meeting in Sandton, and I was running late, and I had just had a phone call from my sister in Durban that left me worried. I sat down. The train started moving. I closed my eyes for a second. And before I knew what I was doing, I was praying.

Quietly. Under my breath. The way people pray in hospitals and in queues and in the back of a church service. The woman sitting next to me glanced at me for a second, and then went back to her phone. The train pulled into Sandton. I got off. The prayer had lasted ninety seconds, and it was the most honest prayer I had said all week.

That was the beginning of what I now call commuter prayer. It has changed the way I use the thirty minutes I spend on the Gautrain every morning. It has changed the way several of the people in our Wednesday Bible study community use their commute. This post is about how to do it well, without making it performative, without making it weird, and without making the person sitting next to you uncomfortable.

## The South African Commute Is a Hidden Gift

The average commute in Gauteng is one of the longest in the country. According to the 2024 National Household Travel Survey, the average Gauteng resident spends between forty-five minutes and ninety minutes a day commuting to and from work. People in the northern suburbs — Centurion, Midrand, Boksburg, the West Rand — often spend two hours each way. The Sandton professional commuting from a townhouse in Paulshof to an office in the CBD will spend close to three hours a day in a car.

That is three hours a day. Twenty-one hours a week. Over a thousand hours a year. It is, in the life of the average South African, one of the largest single blocks of unstructured time that exists.

Most of us use that time for podcasts. For audiobooks. For WhatsApp voice notes. For phone calls to family. For the news. For silence. None of these uses is wrong. All of them are honest.

The question this post is asking is a slightly different one. Could some of that commute time be prayer time? Could the most underused hour of the South African day become the most useful hour of the South African day?

## Why Commuter Prayer Works in Johannesburg Specifically

There are three reasons commuter prayer works particularly well in Johannesburg.

### 1. The Commute Is Long Enough

A ten-minute drive to the office does not give you much room for prayer. A ninety-minute Gautrain ride does. The South African commute is long enough to make commuter prayer practical. You do not have to cram your prayer into a slot that is too small. The slot is already there.

### 2. The Commute Is Regular Enough

The South African commute happens every weekday at roughly the same time. The regularity matters because prayer is a habit, and habits work best when they are anchored to a recurring time of day. The morning Gautrain at 7:15am becomes the same prayer time, five days a week. The afternoon drive from Sandton to Pretoria East at 5:45pm becomes a second prayer time, in the same week.

### 3. The Commute Has Built-in Transitions

Prayer works best when there is a clear beginning and a clear end. The South African commute has both. The Gautrain doors closing. The Uber starting. The taxi leaving the rank. The car starting in the driveway. The transition is built in. When the transition ends — when the Gautrain pulls into Park Station, when the Uber stops outside the office, when the taxi drops you at the corner — the prayer has a natural close.

## A Practical Commuter Prayer Method for Johannesburg

Here is the method several of us in the Bible study community in Parkmore have adopted over the last few years. It is built for a thirty-to-ninety-minute commute, but most of it scales up or down.

### Step 1 — Set the Phone for the Commute

When you get on the Gautrain, or into the Uber, or into the car, put your phone on silent. Do not put it on airplane mode yet. You may need it for navigation or for an emergency. But turn off the notifications. The phone is the biggest competitor for the prayer time. Removing it from the room makes the prayer time possible.

If you are using an e-reader, the same applies. Kindle, Kobo, Boox — put them away. The prayer time is the prayer time.

### Step 2 — Pick the Passage for the Day

The passage is the same passage the Wednesday Bible study is on. If you have not been to a Bible study in a while, pick one of the daily readings from a Bible-in-a-year plan. We will share the Wednesday passage in the WhatsApp group each week.

Read the passage once, slowly. Out loud if you are alone in a car. Under your breath if you are in a taxi or on the train. Even just in your head if you are in an Uber.

### Step 3 — Pray Through Three Sections

Once you have read the passage, divide your prayer time into three sections. They do not need to be equal. They do not need to be long. They just need to be there.

**The first section is for the day you are about to have.** Pray for the meeting you are about to walk into. Pray for the colleague who is hard to work with. Pray for the report that is due on Friday. Pray for the patient you are about to see, or the client you are about to call, or the student you are about to teach.

**The second section is for the people on your list.** Pray for the names on the prayer list in your phone. Pray for your mother. Pray for your friend who is in hospital. Pray for the couple you know who are struggling. Pray for the people in your Bible study community who have asked you to pray for them.

**The third section is for yourself.** Pray for the temptation you are carrying. Pray for the anxiety that woke you up at 4am. Pray for the marriage that is hard. Pray for the decision you cannot make. Pray for the part of yourself you do not show to anyone.

### Step 4 — End with a Short Psalm

End the prayer time with a Psalm. Psalm 23 if you want comfort. Psalm 46 if you are anxious. Psalm 121 if you are tired. Psalm 139 if you need to remember that you are known. Psalm 4 if you need to sleep.

Read it out loud. Read it under your breath. Read it in your head. The Psalm ends the prayer time the way a hymn ends a church service. It sends you into the day.

## How to Do It Without Making It Weird

This is the part most guides on commuter prayer skip. It is the part that matters most.

### In a Car Alone

If you are driving alone, pray out loud. The car is your private room. Pray at the volume you would use to talk to a passenger. It feels strange the first time. By the third time, it feels normal. By the thirtieth time, it feels like the only way to start the day.

### In a Car With Others

If you are carpooling, do not pray out loud. Pray silently. Use the time to read the passage and to hold the people on your prayer list in your heart. The carpool is a community space. Respect it.

### In an Uber

Uber drivers in Johannesburg are some of the most prayer-aware people in the country. If you feel moved to pray out loud in an Uber, the driver has almost certainly heard it before. But if you would rather not, the silent version works just as well.

If you have a regular Uber driver who knows you are a Christian, you can ask them if they would like you to pray for them. We know of at least three Uber drivers in the Sandton-to-Rosebank corridor who are now on the prayer lists of our Wednesday Bible study community, because the rider in the back seat asked.

### On the Gautrain

The Gautrain has a quiet coach. The quiet coach is, predictably, the best place in Johannesburg to pray. If you are in the quiet coach, you can pray silently. If you are in the regular coach, you can pray silently too. The Gautrain is loud enough that even an under-the-breath prayer will not disturb anyone.

### In a Taxi

A taxi in Johannesburg is a community space. The right way to pray in a taxi is to pray for the other people in the taxi. Pray for the driver. Pray for the person next to you. Pray for the country. The taxi is one of the most underused prayer spaces in South Africa.

If you are a regular on a particular taxi route, you will start to know the other regulars. After a few months, you will know their names. After a year, you will know their stories. After a few years, you will know that the woman who gets on at Bosmont every morning is praying for her mother in Limpopo, and you will be praying for her too, because she told you so one morning in isiSotho.

This is what commuter prayer becomes, in the long run. It becomes a way of praying for the people you already see every day.

## A Commuter Prayer for the Week

If you would like a prayer to start with on Monday morning, here is one we use on the Wednesday Bible study WhatsApp group at the beginning of each week.

> *God of the morning commute,*
>
> *Thank you for the day ahead. Thank you for the work you have given us, even on the days we are tired of it. Thank you for the train, the car, the taxi, the Uber — for the way they carry us to the people who need us.*
>
> *We pray for the meeting we are about to walk into. We pray for the colleague who will make us frustrated, and for the one who will surprise us with kindness. We pray for the patient, the client, the student, the customer, the stranger.*
>
> *We pray for the people we have agreed to pray for this week. We hold them in our hearts as we travel. We ask that you would carry them when we cannot.*
>
> *We pray for ourselves. For the part of us that is afraid. For the part of us that is tired. For the part of us that wants to give up and the part that wants to keep going.*
>
> *May this commute be more than a commute. May it be a chapel. May it be a place where we meet you between home and work, between yesterday and tomorrow, between who we have been and who you are making us.*
>
> *We pray in the name of the one who walked the road to Emmaus with two disciples who did not recognise him.*
> *Amen.*

## A Note on Commuter Prayer and the Bible Study Community

One of the unexpected things that has happened in our Wednesday Bible study over the last three years is that several members have started to mention their commuter prayer during the Wednesday discussion. Someone will say *"this morning, on the Gautrain, the verse about the sparrow really hit me."* Someone else will say *"I was praying about this in the car yesterday."* The Wednesday Bible study has become, in a small way, a place where the commuter prayer time gets reflected back into the community.

If you are part of a Bible study community in Johannesburg, or in any South African city, the commuter prayer time does not have to be separate from the Bible study. The two reinforce each other. The morning prayer prepares the heart for the Wednesday discussion. The Wednesday discussion shapes the next morning's prayer. The cycle continues.
