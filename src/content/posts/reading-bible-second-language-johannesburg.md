## The Wednesday Night the Room Laughed Together

A few months ago, in the middle of a Wednesday evening Bible study in Parkmore, a man named Sipho read a sentence out loud from the English Bible on the screen. He paused, looked at the camera, and said: *"I have read this sentence forty times. Tonight is the first time I understood it."*

The room laughed. Not at Sipho. With Sipho. Because every person in the room had a version of Sipho's story. The South African who grew up speaking isiZulu at home and now reads the Bible in English because the Bible study is in English. The Setswana speaker from Mafikeng who joined our Wednesday group last year and has been quietly Googling words between meetings. The Afrikaans mother of three from Centurion who knows her Bible in Afrikaans and is now reading it in English for the first time. The Korean member who has been working through Philippians in two languages for six months.

This post is for them. It is also for every pastor, Bible study leader, and Christian in Johannesburg who has ever watched a person hesitate to speak because they were worried about their English.

If you are not a native English speaker, your Bible reading is not broken. It is bilingual. And bilingual Bible reading is one of the richest things a Christian in Johannesburg can do.

## Why This Matters in Johannesburg Specifically

Johannesburg is the most linguistically dense city in southern Africa. The 2022 census recorded isiZulu as the most spoken language at home, followed by English, Afrikaans, Sesotho, isiXhosa, and Setswana. By the time you add the Portuguese-speaking communities from Mozambique and Angola, the French speakers from the DRC and the francophone African countries, the Swahili speakers from East Africa, the Arabic speakers, the Hindi and Gujarati speakers, the Yoruba and Igbo speakers, the Korean and Mandarin speakers, the Shona speakers, and the Amharic and Tigrinya speakers, the city has well over a hundred home languages in regular use.

Most Bible study groups in Johannesburg operate primarily in English. This is not a mistake. It is a practical choice. English is the working language of the city, of the schools, of the workplace, of the Wednesday Bible study in Parkmore. But the working language is not always the heart language. The heart language — the language a person prays in, dreams in, argues in, grieves in — is often something else.

The Bible study community that ignores this gap is missing the most important part of the room. The Bible study community that makes space for it is one of the most powerful forms of Christian community in Johannesburg today.

## The Five Honest Challenges of Reading the Bible in a Second Language

Before the practical guide, here are the five honest challenges. They are not problems to be solved. They are realities to be acknowledged.

### 1. Some Words in the Bible Do Not Exist in Your Language

The Bible uses concrete English words like *"atonement," "righteousness," "covenant," "sacrifice," "mercy seat."* Most of these words do not translate cleanly into isiZulu, seSotho, Afrikaans, or any of the other South African languages. The translations that exist are usually long phrases. *"Atonement"* in isiZulu becomes *"ukubuyisana"* — the act of bringing things back together. *"Righteousness"* in Afrikaans becomes *"geregtigheid,"* which most Afrikaans speakers understand but cannot easily explain.

When you read the Bible in your second language and you hit a word like this, you have three options. You can skip it. You can guess at it. You can look it up. None of these options is wrong. All of them are honest.

### 2. Some Sentences Are Longer Than They Need to Be

The English Bible, especially the King James Version and the New King James Version, uses sentence structures that even native English speakers find difficult. *"And it came to pass in those days, that there went out a decree from Caesar Augustus, that all the world should be taxed."* That sentence, translated from the original Greek, is doing a lot of work in one breath. A second-language reader has every right to slow down.

If you are reading the Bible in English as a second language, you have permission to take longer than you think you should. The Bible is not a race. The Bible is a slow conversation with God, and God does not mind waiting.

### 3. Some Passages Assume Cultural Knowledge You Do Not Have

The Bible is full of references to first-century Palestinian agricultural life, to ancient Hebrew temple worship, to Greco-Roman political structures. The English reader assumes you know what a *"phylactery"* is. You probably do not. The second-language reader is at no disadvantage here. The first-language reader is at the same disadvantage. The Bible is a foreign book to most modern readers, regardless of what language they speak.

### 4. Some Translations Are Easier Than Others

If English is your second language, the translation you choose matters. The King James Version is beautiful but uses seventeenth-century English that no one speaks anymore. The New International Version is easier. The New Living Translation is the easiest of the major English translations. The Good News Bible is also a good option for second-language readers, though its paraphrasing sometimes drops important nuances.

There is no single right answer. The right translation is the one that gets you reading. If you find yourself skipping a translation because it feels too hard, try a different one. There is no virtue in suffering through a translation that is keeping you from the Bible.

### 5. You Are Not the Only One in the Room

This is the most important honest challenge. If you are reading the Bible in English as a second language, you may feel like the only one in the room who is struggling. You are not. The Wednesday Bible study in Parkmore has been running for ten years, and in that time we have learned that the people who are most hesitant to speak are usually the ones with the deepest insights. They are hesitant because they are translating the passage in their head, checking their understanding, comparing it to what they have heard before, and trying to say it in a way that does not sound silly. That is a lot of work to do before you press the unmute button.

If you are one of those people, please know that the room wants to hear you. Press the button. Say the thing. The English will be fine. The insight will be better.

## A Practical Bilingual Bible Reading Method

Here is a method we have taught to the Bible study community in Parkmore, and that several of our members have adapted for their own reading. It is built for the bilingual reader, but most of it applies to any serious Bible reader.

### Step 1 — Read in Your Heart Language First

Start with the Bible in the language you speak at home. If that is isiZulu, use the 1983 isiZulu Bible or the more recent *IBhayibheli Elingcwele*. If it is Afrikaans, use the 1933/1953 Afrikaans Bible or the 1983 translation. If it is seSotho, use the 1989 translation. If it is Setswana, use the 1987 translation.

The first read is for understanding. You read it the way you read a letter from a friend. You let the words land in the part of your brain that already knows how to receive them.

### Step 2 — Read the Same Passage in English

Open the same passage in an English translation. The New International Version is a good balance of accuracy and readability. The English Standard Version is also good. The Good News Bible is the easiest.

The second read is for the Wednesday Bible study. You read it the way you read a work email. You are reading for precision. You are reading for the words the rest of the room will use on Wednesday.

### Step 3 — Note the Three Words That Did Not Translate

In a notebook or in your phone, write down three English words from the passage that do not have a clean equivalent in your heart language. These are the words that, when you understand them, will deepen your understanding of the passage.

For example, in Philippians 1:6, the English phrase *"he who began a good work in you will carry it on to completion"* has the word *"completion"* — which in isiZulu would be *"ukuqeda"* and in Afrikaans would be *"voltooiing."* The English word *"completion"* carries the sense of bringing something to its proper end. Your language may have the word, but the Bible study on Wednesday will use the English word. Knowing the English word helps you follow the discussion.

### Step 4 — Find One Sentence That Speaks to You in Each Language

In your heart language, find one sentence that resonates. In English, find one sentence that resonates. They will probably not be the same sentence. That is the point.

The sentence in your heart language is the sentence that goes home with you. The sentence in English is the sentence you bring to the Wednesday Bible study. Both are valid. Both are the Bible.

### Step 5 — Come to the Wednesday Bible Study With Both

When you show up on Wednesday at 7:30pm, bring both translations with you. When the discussion opens, mention the sentence from your heart language. Mention the sentence from English. The room will get to see the passage through your two-language lens, and that lens is usually richer than the one-language lens.

This is the part the Bible study community is missing when it insists on English-only discussion. The room is bilingual by default in Johannesburg. The discussion should reflect that.

## How to Lead a Multilingual Bible Study Well

If you are leading a Bible study in Johannesburg — or anywhere with a multilingual room — here are the seven adjustments that have worked for us.

### 1. Start the Evening in Multiple Languages

Open with a short welcome in the working language. Then add a one-line welcome in any other language spoken by someone in the room. You do not need to be fluent. A simple *"sanibonani,"* *"dumelang,"* *"goeie more"* is enough. The room hears that the leader knows there are other languages.

### 2. Read the Passage in Two Languages

Read the passage in the working language. Then ask someone to read it again in their language. The second reading takes an extra three minutes. It is the most important three minutes of the evening.

### 3. Encourage Translation in the Discussion

When someone makes a key point in their language, ask them — gently — to say it in the working language. When someone makes a key point in the working language, and the room knows it has implications in another language, ask if anyone would like to translate.

### 4. Provide a Translation Glossary for Hard Words

For the Wednesday Bible study in Parkmore, we keep a small Google Doc of the difficult words we encounter in the passage of the week. *"Atonement," "justification," "sanctification," "covenant," "righteousness"* — each with a one-sentence explanation in plain English, and a note on how it has been translated in the major South African languages. New members get the link. Old members refer back to it.

### 5. Be Patient With Silence

If you ask a question and the room is silent, the silence is not a failure. The silence is the room translating the question. Wait for it. The answers come.

### 6. Do Not Pretend to Be Multilingual When You Are Not

If you are the leader and you speak only English, do not pretend to know isiZulu or Afrikaans. Use a translator. Ask a member to translate. The humility of asking for help is one of the most powerful things a Bible study leader can model.

### 7. Treat the Heart Language as the Primary Language for Prayer

The discussion can be in the working language. The prayer should be in the heart language. When the room knows that the prayer time is multilingual by design, the heart language speakers bring their deepest prayers. The working language speakers get the gift of hearing prayers they did not know were being prayed.

## A Short List of Multilingual Bible Resources

For South African readers, here are some practical resources we recommend.

- **The Bible in Your Language** — Bible Society of South Africa publishes the Bible in all eleven official South African languages, plus some additional languages. Most are free to read online at bible.co.za.
- **YouVersion App** — has the Bible in almost every major language, with offline downloads. The audio Bible is particularly useful for second-language readers who want to hear the passage read aloud.
- **The IsiZulu, Sesotho, Setswana, and Afrikaans Side-by-Side Bibles** — published by Bible Society, these editions put the English and the heart language on the same page. Useful for the bilingual reading method above.
- **Multilingual Commentaries** — some study Bibles include notes in multiple languages. The *Tshikane Study Bible* is one example for Setswana speakers.
