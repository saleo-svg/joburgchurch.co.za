## A South African Hospital Is Its Own Country

If you have spent any time in a South African hospital — yours or someone else's — you already know it is its own country. The waiting at casualty at Chris Hani Baragwanath. The drive past Helen Joseph on Empire Road. The plastic chair in the corridor at Charlotte Maxeke. The smell of the tea trolley at Netcare Sandton. The auntie who has brought a Tupperware of pap and wors for the patient. The cousin sleeping in the car because the visiting hours are short. The same cousin who has not yet told the rest of the family how bad it is. The doctor who finally appears two hours after the appointment. The pharmacist at the hospital gate who helps with the chronic medication because the queue inside is too long.

The South African hospital is not the same as the hospital in the brochure. The South African hospital has its own language, its own rhythms, its own kindnesses, and its own particular kinds of suffering.

This post is for the Christian who wants to be useful inside the South African hospital — visiting the sick, praying with the sick, helping the family that is carrying the weight. It is for the Christian who has felt helpless in the corridor at 9pm and has wondered what the Bible has to say to that moment. It is for the Bible study member who has driven a friend to oncology at Sandton Oncology Centre and has wanted to know what to pray on the way home.

## What the Bible Actually Says About Visiting the Sick

The Bible has a great deal to say about visiting the sick. More than most Christians realise.

**Matthew 25:36** — *I was sick and you looked after me.*

**James 5:14-15** — *Is anyone among you sick? Let them call the elders of the church to pray over them and anoint them with oil in the name of the Lord. And the prayer offered in faith will make the sick person well; the Lord will raise them up. If they have sinned, they will be forgiven.*

**Mark 1:30-31** — *Simon's mother-in-law was in bed with a fever, and they told Jesus about her. So he went to her, took her hand and helped her up. The fever left her and she began to wait on them.*

**Acts 9:36-43** — *In Joppa there was a disciple named Tabitha (which is translated Dorcas), who was always doing good and helping the poor. She became sick and died... Peter sent them all out of the room; then he got down on his knees and prayed. Turning toward the dead woman, he said, 'Tabitha, get up.' She opened her eyes, and seeing Peter she sat up.*

**Luke 10:30-35** — The parable of the Good Samaritan. *When he saw him, he took pity on him. He went to him and bandaged his wounds, pouring on oil and wine. Then he put the man on his own donkey, brought him to an inn and took care of him.*

What you notice is that visiting the sick is not presented in the Bible as a nice thing for the extra-committed Christian. It is presented as something close to the centre of what it means to follow Jesus. In Matthew 25, the visit to the sick is one of the things the King will mention at the last judgment. In James 5, the prayer of faith over the sick person is a normal Christian practice, not an extraordinary one. In the parable of the Good Samaritan, the model of the neighbour is the person who stops, bandages, pays, and comes back.

The Bible is not silent about the hospital visit. The Bible is unusually clear about it.

## Why Visiting the Sick in South Africa Is Harder (and More Important) Than in Most Places

### 1. The Distance Between Hospitals

In Johannesburg, the specialist hospitals are clustered in a few areas — Parktown, Sandton, Houghton. The patients often live an hour or more away. The family that visits has to choose between spending R200 on petrol for the round trip or spending it on the chronic medication. Several people in our Wednesday Bible study community have figured out the bus routes between Soweto and Charlotte Maxeke, between Tembisa and the oncology unit at Steve Biko, between Mamelodi and Unitas. The visiting is a logistics problem before it is a kindness problem.

### 2. The Public Ward Reality

Many South Africans are admitted to public wards — six beds to a room, curtains that do not quite close, two visiting hours a day, the family bringing the food because the hospital kitchen does not feed the patient the way the family would. The visitors are doing more than visiting. The visitors are feeding, washing, advocating with the nurse, fetching the medication from the pharmacy, calling the specialist on the cell phone because the specialist has not appeared on the ward round. The visit is a small piece of a much larger care operation that the family is running from the plastic chair.

### 3. The Visitor's Own Fear

Many people do not visit the sick because they are afraid. They are afraid of the hospital itself. They are afraid of seeing the relative looking worse than expected. They are afraid of saying the wrong thing. They are afraid of the long corridor and the smell and the beeping. The fear is real. The Bible does not dismiss the fear. The Bible says in 2 Timothy 1:7 — *For the Spirit God gave us does not make us timid, but gives us power, love and self-discipline.* The fear is the entry point. The visit is the move on the other side of the fear.

### 4. The Caregiver's Loneliness

The caregiver in South Africa — the daughter who has given up the job to look after the parent, the wife who is now running the household alone because the husband is at the hospital for the third admission this year, the retired teacher who is now full-time nurse — the caregiver is often lonelier than the patient. The caregiver is the one who has not been visited. The caregiver is the one who has not been prayed with. The caregiver is the one who needs the call, the meal, the afternoon off, the prayer more than the patient does.

## Six Bible-Based Practices for the South African Hospital Visit

These are the practices that several members of our Wednesday Bible study community in Sandton have developed over the years. They are not inventions. They are applications of what the Bible already teaches about visiting the sick.

### Practice 1 — Call Before You Go

Call the family before you drive to the hospital. Ask which ward. Ask what time the doctor is likely to round. Ask whether the patient is up to visitors. Ask whether the family needs anything brought in. The call is the first visit. The call saves the family an hour of waiting for you in the wrong ward.

### Practice 2 — Bring Something the Patient Actually Wants

The Tupperware of food is more important than the flowers. The chronic medication from the pharmacy is more useful than the card. The phone charger is more useful than the magazine. The Bible passage the patient has been asking about is more useful than the sermon. Ask the family what the patient actually needs before you go.

### Practice 3 — Sit, Do Not Just Visit

The visit is not the visit. The sitting is the visit. Sit on the plastic chair. Sit on the edge of the bed if the nurse allows it. Sit in the corridor if the patient is in isolation. Sit for the full visiting hour. The sitting is what the family remembers. The sitting is what the patient remembers. The sitting is what the Bible calls *being present*.

### Practice 4 — Pray Briefly, Specifically, Out Loud

The hospital visit is not the place for the long prayer. The hospital visit is the place for the short prayer, prayed out loud at the bedside, named after the specific thing the family has just told you about. *Lord, we ask you for the blood test tomorrow. We ask you for the doctor who will be on rounds. We ask you for the night nurse. We ask you for the pain to be manageable.* The short prayer is what the Bible means by *the prayer of faith*. The short prayer is the prayer the family will hold onto for the rest of the week.

### Practice 5 — Help With the Logistics

The visit is not complete until you have helped with the small thing on the way out. Drive the car around to the entrance. Take the laundry home to wash. Drop the chronic script at the pharmacy on the way back. Phone the cousin who could not come and give them the update. The logistics help is the kind of help the Good Samaritan gave. The logistics help is the kind of help that matters more than the visit itself.

### Practice 6 — Keep Visiting When the News Gets Worse

The hardest visit is the second one. The second visit is harder than the first. The first visit has the energy of the decision. The second visit has the weight of the diagnosis. The visit after the news has got worse — the visit when the patient is no longer able to talk, the visit when the family is making the decisions — that visit is the most important visit of all. The Bible says in Job 2:13 — *Then they sat down with him on the ground for seven days and seven nights. No one said a word to him, because they saw that his suffering was very great.* The sitting is the visit. The sitting is what the Bible means by friendship.

## What to Pray at the Hospital Bed

Many of us in the Bible study have struggled with what to pray at the bedside. The struggle is not a lack of words. The struggle is a fear of saying the wrong thing. Here are the prayers that have helped.

**For the patient:**
*Lord Jesus, you are the Great Physician. You are the one who went to the home of Simon's mother-in-law and took her by the hand and raised her up. We ask you to be present in this bed, in this ward, in this hospital. We ask you to give the doctors wisdom. We ask you to give the nurses patience. We ask you to give the patient rest. We ask you to do what only you can do.*

**For the family:**
*God of the long corridor, we pray for the daughter who is at the bedside every day. We pray for the husband who is sleeping in the car. We pray for the mother who is also sick at home and cannot be here. Give them strength for the next hour. Give them strength for the next day. Give them the wisdom to know what to say and what to leave unsaid.*

**For the doctor:**
*Lord, we ask you for the doctor on rounds today. We ask you for the specialist who is making the decision. We ask you for the clarity they need. We ask you for the humility to listen to the patient. We ask you for the wisdom that no training can give.*

**For the moment when the news is bad:**
*Lord, we do not have the words. We do not have the right prayer. We ask you to pray in us the prayer we cannot pray (Romans 8:26). Be with the patient. Be with the family. Be with us. Carry us when we cannot carry ourselves.*

## A Prayer for the South African Hospital Visitor

If you are driving to the hospital this week — yours, a parent's, a friend's, a Bible study member's — here is a prayer.

> *God of the hospital bed,*
>
> *We pray for the ward we are driving to. We pray for the patient in the bed. We pray for the family in the plastic chairs. We pray for the nurse who has been on shift since yesterday. We pray for the doctor who is somewhere between this ward and the next.*
>
> *We confess that we are afraid. We confess that we do not know what to say. We confess that the corridor is long and the smell is unfamiliar and the beeping is the sound of a world we cannot control.*
>
> *Give us the courage to drive there. Give us the wisdom to ask before we speak. Give us the grace to sit for the full hour. Give us the words for the short prayer at the bedside.*
>
> *Help us to bring the food the family needs. Help us to help with the logistics on the way out. Help us to keep visiting when the news gets worse.*
>
> *We pray for the patient whose name we are carrying. We pray for the family who is tired. We pray for the doctor who is making the decision. We pray for the morning. We pray for the night.*
>
> *In the name of the one who took the mother-in-law by the hand, who raised the daughter of Jairus from the bed, who himself lay in the borrowed tomb.*
> *Amen.*
